Training on Auditing Serious Irregularities (TGS): Key Lessons from the AG Office

Training on Auditing Serious Irregularities (TGS): Key Lessons from the AG Office#

On 26 June 2025, officials from the Accountant General (AG) Office conducted a training-cum-discussion on Serious Irregularities (TGS). The session focused on strengthening the ability of auditors to identify, investigate, document, and report irregularities that have significant financial, legal, or governance implications.

The discussion emphasized that auditors should move beyond routine compliance checking and adopt a risk-based approach to detect irregularities that may involve fraud, corruption, deliberate misrepresentation, or systemic failures.

Why Serious Irregularities Matter#

Not every audit observation qualifies as a serious irregularity.

A serious irregularity generally involves one or more of the following:

  • Fraud or corruption
  • Deliberate concealment of facts
  • Misappropriation of government funds
  • Financial loss to the Government
  • Abuse of official position
  • Non-compliance resulting in significant consequences
  • Systemic weaknesses affecting governance

Such cases require prompt reporting because they may lead to:

  • Departmental proceedings
  • Criminal investigations
  • Recovery of government money
  • Forensic examination
  • Policy reforms

Objectives of the Training#

The session aimed to help auditors:

  • Understand what constitutes a serious irregularity.
  • Learn methods for identifying high-risk transactions.
  • Design suitable audit procedures.
  • Improve documentation and evidence collection.
  • Understand reporting protocols.
  • Appreciate the role of forensic audit.
  • Recommend corrective and preventive measures.

Characteristics of Serious Irregularities#

The resource persons explained that serious irregularities generally involve one or more of the following characteristics.

1. Lack of Transparency#

Transactions intentionally kept outside established procedures.

Examples include:

  • Non-maintenance of records
  • Concealment of documents
  • Missing approvals
  • Absence of supporting evidence

2. Deliberate Misrepresentation#

This involves knowingly presenting false information.

Examples include:

  • Inflated expenditure
  • Under-reporting revenue
  • False utilisation certificates
  • Manipulation of financial statements

Unlike ordinary errors, deliberate misrepresentation is intentional.

3. Use of Fake Documents#

Fake or fabricated documents are common indicators of fraud.

Examples discussed included:

  • Fake Aadhaar cards
  • Forged certificates
  • Fabricated vouchers
  • False identity documents
  • Altered invoices

4. Financial Fraud#

Financial fraud often involves illegal gain through misuse of government resources.

Examples discussed included:

  • Fraudulent Kisan Credit Card transactions
  • Fake beneficiaries
  • Unauthorized withdrawals
  • Diversion of funds

Examples Discussed During the Session#

Several practical examples were used to explain how serious irregularities arise.

Fraudulent Financial Transactions#

Examples included:

  • Employees obtaining financial benefits using fake documents.
  • Fraudulent claims under government schemes.
  • Misuse of subsidies.

Procurement Irregularities#

The session highlighted common procurement issues such as:

  • No Notice Inviting Tender (NIT)
  • Quotations obtained from preferred vendors only
  • Conflict of interest
  • Splitting of purchases
  • Violation of procurement rules

Such practices reduce transparency and increase the risk of corruption.

Mismanagement of Revenue#

One example involved departments failing to implement provisions of Acts or Rules that could generate government revenue.

Illustrations included:

  • Failure to collect irrigation tax.
  • Failure to enforce revenue provisions.
  • Weak monitoring mechanisms.

Bypassing Administrative Procedures#

Examples included:

  • Junior officials directly approaching higher authorities.
  • Ignoring prescribed approval hierarchy.
  • Unauthorized decision-making.

Such deviations weaken internal controls.

Seven Key Steps in Handling Serious Irregularities#

The training presented a structured framework.

Step 1: Identification#

The first responsibility of an auditor is to identify indicators of serious irregularities.

Possible red flags include:

  • Unusual transactions
  • Missing files
  • Fake documents
  • Large cash payments
  • Unsupported expenditure
  • Sudden increase in expenditure
  • Repeated payments to the same vendor

Large-value transactions should receive greater scrutiny.

Random verification should also be performed.

Step 2: Design Appropriate Audit Procedures#

Once risk areas are identified, audit procedures should be tailored accordingly.

Possible procedures include:

  • Detailed scrutiny
  • Third-party confirmation
  • Bank reconciliation
  • Physical verification
  • Verification of approvals
  • Review of electronic records
  • Cross-checking departmental databases

The emphasis should always be on obtaining sufficient and appropriate audit evidence.

Step 3: Reporting#

Timely reporting is critical.

Depending upon the seriousness, the matter may require reporting to:

  • Head of Office
  • Finance Department
  • Administrative Department
  • Accountant General
  • Vigilance Authorities
  • Police authorities (where criminal offences are suspected)

The training stressed that auditors should report facts supported by evidence and avoid speculative conclusions.

Step 4: Special Audit#

Certain cases require specialized examination.

Special audits may involve:

  • Experts
  • Technical specialists
  • Chartered accountants
  • Departmental specialists

The objective is to determine the complete extent of irregularity.

Step 5: Impact Assessment#

The consequences of serious irregularities extend beyond monetary loss.

Possible impacts include:

Financial Impact#

  • Loss of government revenue
  • Wasteful expenditure
  • Inadmissible payments

Legal Impact#

  • Criminal prosecution
  • Departmental action
  • Recovery proceedings

Institutional Impact#

  • Loss of public confidence
  • Damage to departmental reputation
  • Weak governance

The training also referred to situations where funding agencies may impose penalties if serious financial irregularities are established.

Step 6: Forensic Audit#

Where fraud is suspected, forensic audit becomes essential.

Unlike routine audit, forensic audit focuses on evidence that can withstand legal scrutiny.

Important evidence includes:

  • Original files
  • Note sheets
  • Official correspondence
  • Digital records
  • Geo-tagged photographs
  • Audio recordings
  • Video recordings
  • System logs
  • Bank records

Proper preservation of evidence is extremely important.

Step 7: Follow-Up#

Audit does not end with reporting.

Effective follow-up includes:

  • Corrective action
  • Recovery of funds
  • Strengthening internal controls
  • Departmental proceedings
  • Suspension where necessary
  • Policy improvements
  • Monitoring compliance

The ultimate objective is preventing recurrence.

Importance of Risk Assessment#

Risk assessment should drive the audit process.

Auditors should identify:

  • High-risk departments
  • High-value schemes
  • Weak internal controls
  • Revenue-sensitive functions
  • Procurement-intensive activities

A risk-based audit improves efficiency and increases the likelihood of detecting serious irregularities.

Internal Controls#

The training emphasized that effective internal controls reduce opportunities for fraud.

Important controls include:

  • Proper segregation of duties
  • Bank reconciliation
  • Periodic verification
  • Approval hierarchy
  • Handover procedures
  • Asset verification
  • Access controls

Auditors should evaluate whether these controls are:

  • Adequate
  • Functioning properly
  • Regularly monitored

Principles of Compliance Audit#

Reference was made to the principles governing compliance audit.

The discussion highlighted the following audit principles.

Legality#

All actions should comply with:

  • Acts
  • Rules
  • Government Orders
  • Notifications
  • Circulars

Adequacy#

Internal control systems should be sufficient to safeguard public resources.

Transparency#

Government decisions should be transparent.

Records should be:

  • Properly maintained
  • Easily traceable
  • Fully documented

Propriety and Prudence#

Auditors should also assess whether expenditure demonstrates:

  • Financial wisdom
  • Economy
  • Good governance
  • Public interest

Even if expenditure is technically legal, it may still violate the principles of propriety.

Effectiveness#

Government spending should produce intended outcomes.

Questions an auditor should ask include:

  • Were objectives achieved?
  • Were resources used efficiently?
  • Was public money spent economically?
  • Were expected benefits delivered?

Common Categories of Serious Irregularities#

The session broadly classified serious irregularities into the following groups.

1. Fraud and Corruption#

Examples:

  • Embezzlement
  • Bribery
  • Fake claims
  • Misappropriation
  • Deliberate falsification

2. Non-Compliance#

Examples:

  • Procurement violations
  • Absence of NIT
  • Ignoring prescribed procedures
  • Tax evasion
  • Conflict of interest

3. Mismanagement of Funds#

Examples:

  • Idle funds
  • Diversion of funds
  • Failure to generate revenue
  • Poor financial management

4. Deviation from Established Procedures#

Examples:

  • Bypassing approval hierarchy
  • Unauthorized sanctions
  • Ignoring financial rules
  • Weak administrative controls

Reporting Serious Irregularities#

The resource persons emphasized that reports should be:

  • Objective
  • Evidence-based
  • Clear
  • Concise
  • Free from assumptions

Auditors should avoid using words such as fraud or corruption unless supported by sufficient evidence.

Where evidence is still being developed, it is safer to describe the issue as a serious irregularity, supported by factual observations.

Value Addition by the AG Office#

The training concluded by explaining that reports submitted by Local Fund Audit offices are further examined by the AG Office.

The AG Office provides:

  • Technical guidance
  • Additional scrutiny
  • Quality assurance
  • Value addition to audit findings
  • Recommendations for further investigation where necessary

This collaborative approach strengthens audit quality and improves accountability.

Key Takeaways#

The session reinforced several important lessons.

  • Serious irregularities require immediate attention.
  • Risk assessment should guide audit planning.
  • Strong evidence is the foundation of every audit finding.
  • Documentation should be complete and reliable.
  • Internal controls must be evaluated continuously.
  • Follow-up is as important as detection.
  • Professional judgment and objectivity are essential throughout the audit process.

Final Thoughts#

Auditing serious irregularities is one of the most demanding responsibilities of a public sector auditor. Detecting such cases requires more than checking vouchers and registers. It demands analytical thinking, professional skepticism, careful documentation, and a thorough understanding of governance systems.

The training provided valuable practical insights into identifying red flags, designing appropriate audit procedures, collecting legally sustainable evidence, and ensuring that audit findings contribute to stronger financial management and public accountability.

For auditors working in Local Fund Audit and related organizations, these principles form the foundation of effective compliance auditing and help safeguard public resources through timely detection and reporting of serious irregularities.
