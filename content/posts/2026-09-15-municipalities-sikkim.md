# Municipalities in Sikkim: History, Structure and Current Situation

Municipalities are urban local governments responsible for civic services in towns and cities. They deal with matters such as sanitation, waste management, roads, drainage, water supply, streetlighting, building regulation, public conveniences and urban development.

Sikkim’s modern urban local-government system developed through several stages:

1. Traditional town administration.
2. The Gangtok Municipality and Corporation legislation.
3. The Sikkim Municipalities Act, 1995.
4. The 74th Constitutional Amendment.
5. The Sikkim Municipalities Act, 2007.
6. Formation and elections of urban local bodies from 2010–11 onwards.
7. Expansion from seven urban local bodies to nine civic bodies by the 2026 municipal election cycle.

As of the 2026 municipal election process, Sikkim had **nine urban local bodies with 63 elected seats**:

- Gangtok Municipal Corporation — 21 wards.
- Namchi Municipal Council — 7 wards.
- Seven Nagar Panchayats — 35 wards in total.

The seven Nagar Panchayats were:

- Singtam.
- Pakyong.
- Rangpo.
- Gyalshing.
- Naya Bazar–Jorethang.
- Soreng.
- Mangan.

The total electorate for the 2026 civic elections was reported as approximately **93,800 voters**, including **47,273 male voters and 46,527 female voters**. # Part I: Meaning and Importance

## 1. What Is a Municipality?

A municipality is an elected urban local-government institution.

It governs an urban area such as:

- A city.
- A town.
- A transitional urban area.
- A notified urban settlement.

A municipality usually has:

- Elected councillors.
- A chairperson, president or mayor.
- An executive officer or municipal commissioner.
- Municipal employees.
- A municipal fund.
- Statutory functions.
- Responsibility for urban services.

### Simple explanation

A municipality is the government closest to urban residents.

If people need:

- Waste collection.
- Drain cleaning.
- Trade licences.
- Building permission.
- Birth certificates.
- Streetlights.
- Local roads.
- Parking arrangements.

they generally interact with the municipality.

***

## 2. Why Are Municipalities Important?

Urban areas require organised civic services.

A municipal institution helps manage:

- Public health.
- Sanitation.
- Solid waste.
- Drainage.
- Water supply.
- Urban roads.
- Streetlighting.
- Parking.
- Public conveniences.
- Building regulation.
- Markets.
- Birth and death registration.
- Urban poverty alleviation.
- Environmental management.

The 74th Constitutional Amendment gave constitutional recognition to urban local governments and identified **18 subjects** in the Twelfth Schedule. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/webroot/uploads/download_audit_report/2016/Chapter%204-062f8ed5db4b4d6.60637334.pdf)

### Simple example

A State department may construct a major highway, but a municipality generally deals with:

- Local drains.
- Garbage collection.
- Local streetlights.
- Trade licences.
- Building permissions.
- Public toilets.
- Neighbourhood roads.
- Local parking.

# Part II: Constitutional Framework

## 3. The 74th Constitutional Amendment

The **74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992** created the constitutional framework for urban local government.

It inserted:

- Part IX-A of the Constitution.
- Articles 243P to 243ZG.
- The Twelfth Schedule.
- Constitutional recognition for Municipalities.
- Regular elections.
- Five-year terms.
- Reservation for women and disadvantaged communities.
- State Election Commissions.
- State Finance Commissions.
- Ward Committees in larger urban bodies.
- District and Metropolitan Planning Committees.

### Simple explanation

Before the 74th Amendment, urban local bodies existed under State laws but did not have the same constitutional protection and recognition.

The amendment aimed to make Municipalities genuine institutions of local self-government.

***

The Twelfth Schedule contains 18 functions that may be assigned to urban local bodies.

| 3 | Planning for economic and social development |

| 11 | Urban poverty alleviation |

### Important point

The Constitution identifies these subjects, but actual transfer depends on State laws, rules, notifications, funds, staff and administrative arrangements.

# Part III: Historical Development in Sikkim

## 5. Early Urban Administration

Before modern municipal legislation, urban settlements in Sikkim were administered through traditional and government arrangements.

Gangtok gradually developed as the administrative and political centre of Sikkim.

The growth of urban settlements created the need for formal systems dealing with:

- Roads.
- Markets.
- Drainage.
- Sanitation.
- Building control.
- Public health.
- Local taxation.
- Civic infrastructure.

### Simple explanation

As towns became larger, informal administration was no longer enough. Formal municipal institutions became necessary.

***

## 6. The Gangtok Municipal Corporation Act, 1975

The **Gangtok Municipality, Corporation Act, 1975** was an important early municipal law.

The Act provided a legal framework for municipal administration in Gangtok. [prsindia](https://prsindia.org/files/bills_acts/acts_states/sikkim/1975/1975SK4.pdf)

It addressed matters such as:

- Constitution of the municipal authority.
- Municipal administration.
- Public health.
- Sanitation.
- Streets and public places.
- Municipal taxation.
- Licences.
- Municipal funds.
- Regulation of buildings and activities.

### Simple explanation

The 1975 Act was the early legal foundation for municipal administration in Gangtok.

It showed that Gangtok required a separate urban governing structure because of its importance as the State capital.

***

## 7. The Sikkim Municipalities Act, 1995

Sikkim enacted the **Sikkim Municipalities Act, 1995** before the comprehensive 2007 legislation.

The 1995 Act provided an initial statutory framework for municipalities after Sikkim’s constitutional and administrative integration with India.

However, the later **Sikkim Municipalities Act, 2007** became the principal framework for urban local governance in conformity with the 74th Constitutional Amendment.

### Simple explanation

The 1995 Act was an earlier municipal law. The 2007 Act later provided a more comprehensive and constitutionally aligned structure.

***

## 8. The Sikkim Municipalities Act, 2007

The **Sikkim Municipalities Act, 2007** is the principal law governing Municipalities in Sikkim.

It was enacted to provide municipal governance in conformity with the 74th Constitutional Amendment.

The Act applies throughout Sikkim except cantonment areas and allows the State Government to establish municipal areas through notification. [lawsisto](https://lawsisto.com/Read-State-Act/MTI4Ng==/THE-SIKKIM-MUNICIPALITIES-ACT-2007)

The Act provides for:

- Municipal Corporation.
- Municipal Council.
- Nagar Panchayat.
- Wards.
- Councillors.
- Mayor or Chairperson.
- Municipal Commissioner or Executive Officer.
- Municipal funds.
- Taxation and fees.
- Municipal budgets.
- Accounts.
- Audit.
- Committees.
- Elections.
- Municipal functions.
- Government supervision.

### Simple explanation

The 2007 Act is the main legal framework for modern urban local government in Sikkim.

# Part IV: Categories of Urban Local Bodies

## 9. Municipal Corporation

A Municipal Corporation is generally established for a large urban area.

In Sikkim, the main Municipal Corporation is:

- **Gangtok Municipal Corporation**.

The 2007 Act includes a population-based table for determining the number of councillors for different municipal categories. The Municipal Corporation category is associated with urban areas having a population of **70,000 or more**, subject to statutory conditions and notification. [lawsisto](https://lawsisto.com/Read-State-Act/MTI4Ng==/THE-SIKKIM-MUNICIPALITIES-ACT-2007)

### Main features

A Municipal Corporation generally has:

- Mayor.
- Deputy Mayor.
- Councillors.
- Municipal Commissioner.
- Executive officers and technical staff.
- Standing or subject committees.
- Larger municipal jurisdiction.

***

## 10. Municipal Council

A Municipal Council generally governs a smaller but established urban area.

Sikkim’s Municipal Council is:

- **Namchi Municipal Council**.

Earlier municipal arrangements also included municipal councils at places such as:

- Jorethang.
- Geyzing.

Later reorganisation changed the structure and classification of several urban bodies.

### Main features

A Municipal Council generally has:

- Chairperson.
- Vice-Chairperson.
- Councillors.
- Municipal Executive Officer.
- Administrative staff.
- Municipal fund.
- Civic-service responsibilities.

***

## 11. Nagar Panchayat

A Nagar Panchayat is generally established for a transitional area moving from rural to urban characteristics or for a smaller urban area.

The current urban local bodies classified as Nagar Panchayats include:

- Singtam.
- Pakyong.
- Rangpo.
- Gyalshing.
- Naya Bazar–Jorethang.
- Soreng.
- Mangan.

### Main features

A Nagar Panchayat generally has:

- President.
- Vice-President.
- Councillors.
- Municipal Executive Officer.
- Municipal fund.
- Responsibility for basic town services.

### Simple explanation

A Nagar Panchayat is a smaller urban local body, while a Municipal Council or Corporation generally governs a larger urban area.

# Part V: Organisation of Municipalities

## 13. Elected and Administrative Wings

A Municipality has two broad wings:

1. **Elected wing.**
2. **Administrative or executive wing.**

### Elected wing

The elected wing includes:

- Mayor and Deputy Mayor in a Municipal Corporation.
- Chairperson and Vice-Chairperson in a Municipal Council.
- President and Vice-President in a Nagar Panchayat.
- Councillors.

### Administrative wing

The administrative wing includes:

- Municipal Commissioner in a Municipal Corporation.
- Municipal Executive Officer in a Municipal Council or Nagar Panchayat.
- Engineers.
- Sanitation officers.
- Accountants.
- Tax staff.
- Technical and support staff.

The CAG describes the Municipal Commissioner as the executive head of Gangtok Municipal Corporation, while Municipal Executive Officers head Municipal Councils and Nagar Panchayats. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/webroot/uploads/download_audit_report/2016/Chapter%204-062f8ed5db4b4d6.60637334.pdf)

***

## 14. Mayor, Chairperson and President

The elected head provides political and representative leadership.

### Mayor

The Mayor presides over the Municipal Corporation and represents the city.

### Chairperson

The Chairperson presides over a Municipal Council.

### President

The President presides over a Nagar Panchayat.

### Common responsibilities

The elected head generally:

- Presides over meetings.
- Guides municipal policy.
- Represents the municipality.
- Supervises implementation at a broad level.
- Works with committees and officers.
- Supports preparation of budgets and plans.
- Ensures that decisions are recorded.

### Important limitation

The elected head does not personally perform every administrative function.

Financial and administrative powers must be exercised according to:

- The Act.
- Rules.
- Delegation orders.
- Budget approvals.
- Government instructions.
- Municipal resolutions.

***

## 15. Councillors

Councillors represent wards.

Their responsibilities include:

- Raising ward-level issues.
- Participating in meetings.
- Reviewing municipal proposals.
- Discussing budgets.
- Monitoring civic services.
- Participating in committees.
- Communicating public complaints.
- Examining reports.
- Supporting local planning.

### Good councillor oversight

A councillor should ask:

- What is the budget?
- What is the expected result?
- Who is responsible?
- How much has been spent?
- Is the service reaching citizens?
- Are there complaints?
- What does the audit report say?
- What action has been taken?

***

## 16. Municipal Commissioner and Executive Officers

The executive officers manage daily administration.

Their responsibilities may include:

- Implementing council decisions.
- Supervising municipal staff.
- Managing records.
- Preparing budgets.
- Managing the municipal fund.
- Supervising contracts.
- Maintaining assets.
- Coordinating service delivery.
- Preparing financial statements.
- Responding to audit.
- Reporting to the Government.

### Simple explanation

The elected body decides policy and priorities. The executive officer manages implementation and administration according to law.

## 18. Solid-Waste Management

Solid-waste management includes:

1. Waste generation.
2. Segregation.
3. Door-to-door collection.
4. Transportation.
5. Processing.
6. Recycling.
7. Disposal.

### Simple explanation

Municipal responsibility does not end when waste is collected. It includes the complete waste chain.

### Important indicators

$$\text{Collection Coverage}=\frac{\text{Households Served}}{\text{Total Households}}\times 100\]$$

$$\text{Segregation Rate}=\frac{\text{Segregated Waste}}{\text{Total Waste Collected}}\times 100$$

$$\text{Processing Rate}=\frac{\text{Waste Processed}}{\text{Total Waste Collected}}\times 100$$

The CAG’s earlier review of Sikkim ULBs identified service-level benchmarks for waste management, including coverage, segregation, recovery and cost recovery. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/webroot/uploads/download_audit_report/2016/Chapter%204-062f8ed5db4b4d6.60637334.pdf)

# Part VII: Committees and Participation

## 25. Subject Committees

Under Section 27(1) of the Sikkim Municipalities Act, a Municipal Corporation may constitute Subject Committees for matters such as:

- Water supply.
- Drainage and sewerage.
- Solid-waste management.
- Urban environment.
- Land-use control.
- Slum services.

Municipalities may also form ad hoc or joint committees when directed by the State Government. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/webroot/uploads/download_audit_report/2016/Chapter%204-062f8ed5db4b4d6.60637334.pdf)

### Simple explanation

Subject Committees allow councillors with relevant interest or knowledge to examine specialised areas in greater detail.

### Why committees matter

A full council may not have enough time to examine:

- Drainage design.
- Waste-collection routes.
- Environmental issues.
- Slum services.
- Land-use conflicts.

A committee can study the matter and submit recommendations.

***

## 26. Ward-Level Participation

Ward-level participation helps connect the municipality with local residents.

It may involve:

- Ward meetings.
- Public consultations.
- Complaint registers.
- Service monitoring.
- Local development priorities.
- Citizen feedback.
- Resident welfare groups.

### Important caution

The CAG’s earlier review found that committees and ward-level structures had not been adequately constituted or made functional at that time. This indicates the importance of verifying current arrangements rather than assuming that a statutory committee is functioning merely because the law permits it. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/webroot/uploads/download_audit_report/2016/Chapter%204-062f8ed5db4b4d6.60637334.pdf)

# Part VIII: Municipal Finance

## 27. Sources of Municipal Funds

Municipal funds may come from:

- Property tax.
- Building permission fees.
- Trade licence fees.
- Parking fees.
- Solid-waste user charges.
- Water charges.
- Rent from municipal properties.
- Advertisement fees.
- Market fees.
- Fines and penalties.
- State Government grants.
- Central Government grants.
- Finance Commission grants.
- Loans and deposits where authorised.

### Simple explanation

A municipality has two broad financial sources:

1. **Own-source revenue.**
2. **Government transfers and grants.**

***

## 28. Own-Source Revenue

Own-source revenue, or OSR, is money collected by the Municipality through its own taxes, fees and charges.

Examples include:

- Property-related tax.
- Parking fees.
- Trade-licence renewal.
- Solid-waste fees.
- Rent.
- Market fees.
- Building-plan fees.

The CAG’s historical data for 2015–16 showed total own revenue of approximately **₹690.73 lakh** across seven ULBs, including:

- Gangtok Municipal Corporation: ₹429.29 lakh.
- Namchi Municipal Council: ₹61.35 lakh.
- Jorethang Municipal Council: ₹44.22 lakh.
- Geyzing Municipal Council: ₹14.71 lakh.
- Rangpo Nagar Panchayat: ₹44.20 lakh.
- Singtam Nagar Panchayat: ₹68.43 lakh.
- Mangan Nagar Panchayat: ₹28.53 lakh.

 [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/webroot/uploads/download_audit_report/2016/Chapter%204-062f8ed5db4b4d6.60637334.pdf)

### Caution

These are historical figures for 2015–16 and should not be treated as current revenue values.

***

## 29. Why Own Revenue Matters

Strong own-source revenue helps municipalities:

- Maintain assets.
- Pay service costs.
- Respond to emergencies.
- Reduce dependence on grants.
- Improve financial autonomy.
- Plan multi-year projects.
- Provide predictable services.

### Audit questions

- Is the tax base complete?
- Are all properties assessed?
- Are fees revised lawfully?
- Are arrears monitored?
- Are collections deposited promptly?
- Are exemptions authorised?
- Are user charges related to service cost?
- Is revenue spent for the approved purpose?

***

## 30. Government Grants

Municipalities receive grants from:

- State Government.
- Union Government.
- Finance Commissions.
- Urban-development schemes.
- Sanitation missions.
- Water and sewerage programmes.
- Poverty-alleviation schemes.
- Other departments.

The CAG’s historical review recorded that during 2015–16 the seven ULBs received central and State grants of approximately **₹1,230.71 lakh** and incurred expenditure of approximately **₹825.49 lakh**, leaving a balance of **₹405.22 lakh**. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/webroot/uploads/download_audit_report/2016/Chapter%204-062f8ed5db4b4d6.60637334.pdf)

### Audit importance

Unspent grants should be:

- Properly identified.
- Kept in authorised accounts.
- Used only for the approved purpose.
- Carried forward according to rules.
- Returned where required.
- Reflected accurately in the accounts.

***

## 31. State Finance Commission

The State Finance Commission reviews the financial position of:

- Panchayats.
- Municipalities.
- Municipal Councils.
- Nagar Panchayats.

It recommends:

- Distribution of State taxes.
- Assignment of taxes and fees.
- Grants-in-aid.
- Sharing of resources.
- Measures to improve local-body finances.

### Simple explanation

The State Finance Commission helps determine how State-level resources should be shared with local bodies.

***

## 32. Municipal Budget

A municipal budget should show:

- Expected revenue.
- Grants.
- Expenditure.
- Works.
- Salaries.
- Maintenance.
- Service costs.
- Debt or liabilities.
- Scheme-wise spending.

# Part IX: Municipal Accounts and Audit

## 33. Municipal Accounts

Municipalities should prepare financial statements showing:

- Income.
- Expenditure.
- Receipts.
- Payments.
- Assets.
- Liabilities.
- Special funds.
- Grants.
- Bank balances.

- Annual financial statements within **four months** of the close of the financial year.
- Annual balance sheet within **three months** of the close of the financial year.

 [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/webroot/uploads/download_audit_report/2016/Chapter%204-062f8ed5db4b4d6.60637334.pdf)

***

## 34. Municipal Accounting Manual

The National Municipal Accounts Manual promoted accrual-based double-entry accounting for urban local bodies.

Sikkim’s Urban Local Bodies Accounting Manual, or **SULBAM**, was drafted to provide procedures and registers for municipal financial administration.

The CAG’s earlier review reported that the manual had not been approved at that time and that several prescribed registers were not maintained.

Examples of required registers included:

- Demand and collection register.
- Rent register.
- Bill-payment register.
- Movable-property register.
- Dishonoured-cheque register.
- Security-deposit register.
- Deposit-work register.

 [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/webroot/uploads/download_audit_report/2016/Chapter%204-062f8ed5db4b4d6.60637334.pdf)

### Simple explanation

A municipality needs standard accounting rules and registers so that financial information is complete, comparable and auditable.

***

## 35. Primary Municipal Auditor

Under Section 60(1) of the Sikkim Municipalities Act, municipal accounts may be audited by:

- The Director of Local Fund Audit.
- Another person appointed by the State Government.
- A Chartered Accountant appointed from a government-approved panel.

 [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/webroot/uploads/download_audit_report/2016/Chapter%204-062f8ed5db4b4d6.60637334.pdf)

### Simple explanation

The municipality does not have unrestricted freedom to choose any auditor. The auditor must be appointed according to the statutory framework.

***

## 36. Role of the CAG

The State Government entrusted audit of all ULBs to the CAG under a **Technical Guidance and Support arrangement in June 2011**, under Section 20(1) of the CAG’s Act.

Audit by the Accountant General, Sikkim began from **2012–13** under this arrangement. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/webroot/uploads/download_audit_report/2016/Chapter%204-062f8ed5db4b4d6.60637334.pdf)

The CAG may:

- Provide technical guidance.
- Conduct test checks.
- Review municipal accounts.
- Examine internal controls.
- Conduct performance and compliance audit.
- Issue Inspection Reports.
- Report serious issues to the State Government and Legislature where applicable.

## 45. Service-Level Benchmarks

A service-level benchmark is a measurable standard for municipal performance.

Examples:

| Service | Possible indicator |
|---|---|
| Water supply | Hours of supply per day |
| Solid waste | Percentage of households covered |
| Drainage | Percentage of drains functional |
| Streetlighting | Percentage of lights working |
| Roads | Percentage maintained to standard |
| Complaints | Percentage resolved within time |
| Revenue | Collection efficiency |
| Birth registration | Time taken to issue certificate |
| Waste processing | Percentage of collected waste processed |

### Simple explanation

A benchmark tells citizens and administrators what level of service should be delivered.

Without a benchmark, a municipality may claim success merely because some work was done.

## 51. Unspent Grants and Utilisation Certificates

 [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/webroot/uploads/download_audit_report/2016/Chapter%204-062f8ed5db4b4d6.60637334.pdf)

### Why this is serious

A utilisation certificate is a formal statement that funds have been used for the approved purpose.

An exaggerated or unsupported certificate may lead to:

- Incorrect financial reporting.
- Release of further grants without proper basis.
- Misstatement of expenditure.
- Weak accountability.
- Possible recovery or disciplinary action.

### Control measures

- Reconcile UCs with cashbook and bank statements.
- Obtain technical completion certificates.
- Verify physical assets.
- Require officer certification.
- Review UCs independently before submission.

# Part XIII: Municipalities and the 3Es

## 55. Economy

Economy asks:

- Were inputs bought at reasonable prices?
- Were quantities based on actual need?
- Were specifications suitable?
- Was procurement competitive?
- Were life-cycle costs considered?

### Example

A municipality buys garbage vehicles at ₹30 lakh each, although suitable vehicles are available at ₹24 lakh each.

The auditor should examine:

$$\text{Possible Price Difference}=₹30,00,000 - ₹24,00,000=₹6,00,000 \text{ per vehicle}$$

The difference may be justified by quality or capacity, but documentary support is required.

***

## 56. Efficiency

Efficiency asks:

- Were vehicles used?
- Were waste routes completed?
- Were workers deployed properly?
- Were projects completed within time?
- Were materials wasted?
- Were assets idle?
- Was service delivered at reasonable unit cost?

### Example

A municipality purchases five waste vehicles, but two remain idle because drivers are unavailable.

The purchase may be:

- Economically weak because operational costs were not considered.
- Inefficient because assets are unused.
- Ineffective because waste collection does not improve.

***

## 57. Effectiveness

Effectiveness asks:

- Did the service reach residents?
- Did pollution reduce?
- Did roads improve?
- Did waste get processed?
- Did water supply become reliable?
- Did citizen complaints decline?
- Did the municipality achieve the intended objective?

### Example

A municipality constructs a waste-processing facility, but all collected waste continues to be dumped untreated.

The facility exists as an output, but the waste-management outcome is not achieved.

# Part XVIII: Simple Summary of the Current Situation

## 74. What Is the Present Position?

In simple language:

- Sikkim has a constitutionally recognised urban local-government system.
- The Sikkim Municipalities Act, 2007 is the main law.
- Gangtok is governed by a Municipal Corporation.
- Namchi is governed by a Municipal Council.
- Seven smaller towns are governed by Nagar Panchayats.
- There are nine urban local bodies in the 2026 election structure.
- The municipal system has expanded since the seven-ULB structure reported in 2016.
- Civic elections were held under the State’s current municipal framework.
- Urban bodies are responsible for sanitation, waste, roads, drainage and other civic services.
- Municipalities receive both own-source revenue and government grants.
- Audit is performed through the DLFA and CAG-related arrangements.

# Part XIX: Conclusion

Municipalities in Sikkim have developed from earlier town-administration arrangements into constitutionally recognised urban local governments.

The important legal milestones are:

1. Gangtok municipal legislation in 1975.
2. The Sikkim Municipalities Act, 1995.
3. The 74th Constitutional Amendment.
4. The Sikkim Municipalities Act, 2007.
5. Formation of the modern ULB structure in 2010–11.
6. Expansion and reorganisation of urban local bodies by 2026.

The present system includes:

- One Municipal Corporation.
- One Municipal Council.
- Seven Nagar Panchayats.
- Sixty-three municipal seats.
- Approximately 93,800 voters in the 2026 civic election cycle.

The future strength of municipal governance will depend on whether ULBs receive genuine control over:

- Functions.
- Funds.
- Functionaries.
- Assets.
- Planning.
- Service standards.
- Revenue administration.

For citizens, an effective municipality is one that keeps streets clean, drains functional, lights working, records accurate, permits transparent and public money properly used.

For auditors, the central questions are:

> Was the money legally spent?  
> Was it spent economically and efficiently?  
> Did municipal services actually improve the lives of citizens?
