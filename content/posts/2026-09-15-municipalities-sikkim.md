# Municipalities in Sikkim: History, Structure and Current Situation

Municipalities are urban local governments responsible for civic services in towns and cities. They administer sanitation, waste management, roads, drainage, water supply, streetlighting, building regulation, public conveniences and urban planning.

Sikkim’s modern urban local government system developed through distinct historical and legislative stages:

1. Traditional town administration under monarchy and early protectorate arrangements.
2. The Gangtok Municipality and Corporation legislation of 1975.
3. The Sikkim Municipalities Act, 1995.
4. Implementation of the 74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992.
5. Enactment of the comprehensive Sikkim Municipalities Act, 2007.
6. Formation and formal democratic elections of Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) from 2010–11 onwards.
7. Expansion from a seven-body configuration to nine civic bodies by the 2026 municipal election cycle.

As of the 2026 municipal election process, Sikkim comprised **nine urban local bodies with 63 elected seats**:

* Gangtok Municipal Corporation: 21 wards.
* Namchi Municipal Council: 7 wards.
* Seven Nagar Panchayats: 35 wards in total.

The seven Nagar Panchayats are:

* Singtam.
* Pakyong.
* Rangpo.
* Gyalshing.
* Naya Bazar–Jorethang.
* Soreng.
* Mangan.

The total electorate for the 2026 civic elections was reported as approximately **93,800 voters**, comprising **47,273 male voters and 46,527 female voters**.

## 1. Concept and Role of Municipalities

An urban local body is a constitutionally sanctioned, elected local governing authority. It administers designated urban boundaries categorized as:

* A larger urban area (Municipal Corporation).
* A smaller urban area (Municipal Council).
* A transitional urban area (Nagar Panchayat).

A functional municipality comprises:

* An elected council of ward representatives.
* A presiding political executive (Mayor, Chairperson or President).
* An administrative executive authority (Municipal Commissioner or Executive Officer).
* Permanent municipal technical, revenue and sanitary cadres.
* A dedicated Municipal Fund established by statute.
* Legally defined civic and regulatory powers.

Municipalities constitute the tier of public administration closest to urban citizens, providing foundational services including solid waste management, stormwater drainage, trade licensing, building permits, civil registration, local street maintenance and parking management.

## 2. Constitutional Framework: The 74th Amendment

The **74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992** established the nationwide framework for urban local self-government by inserting Part IX-A (Articles 243P to 243ZG) and the Twelfth Schedule into the Constitution of India.

The amendment mandates:

* Constitutional recognition of three municipal tiers: Nagar Panchayats, Municipal Councils and Municipal Corporations (Article 243Q).
* Mandatory five-year terms with elections conducted prior to dissolution (Article 243U).
* Reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and women (Article 243T).
* Independent election oversight by the State Election Commission (Article 243K and 243ZA).
* Review of municipal financial distribution by the State Finance Commission (Article 243I and 243Y).
* Constitution of Ward Committees in urban areas with populations of 3 lakh or more (Article 243S).
* District Planning Committees (Article 243ZD) and Metropolitan Planning Committees (Article 243ZE).

### The Twelfth Schedule

Article 243W authorizes State Legislatures to endow municipalities with powers and responsibilities regarding the 18 functional matters enumerated in the Twelfth Schedule:

| No. | Functional Subject |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Urban planning, including town planning |
| 2 | Regulation of land use and construction of buildings |
| 3 | Planning for economic and social development |
| 4 | Roads and bridges |
| 5 | Water supply for domestic, industrial and commercial purposes |
| 6 | Public health, sanitation, conservancy and solid-waste management |
| 7 | Fire services |
| 8 | Urban forestry, protection of the environment and promotion of ecological aspects |
| 9 | Safeguarding the interests of weaker sections of society, including persons with disabilities |
| 10 | Slum improvement and upgradation |
| 11 | Urban poverty alleviation |
| 12 | Provision of urban amenities and facilities such as parks, gardens and playgrounds |
| 13 | Promotion of cultural, educational and aesthetic aspects |
| 14 | Burials and burial grounds, cremations, cremation grounds and electric crematoriums |
| 15 | Cattle pounds; prevention of cruelty to animals |
| 16 | Vital statistics, including registration of births and deaths |
| 17 | Public amenities, including streetlighting, parking lots, bus stops and public conveniences |
| 18 | Regulation of slaughterhouses and tanneries |

The Twelfth Schedule serves as an enabling framework. The operational transfer of these subjects requires executive notification, statutory transfer of assets and personnel, and budgetary devolution by the State Government.

## 3. Historical Development in Sikkim

### Early Urban Administration

Prior to modern municipal legislation, urban settlements in Sikkim were administered directly through state departments, bazaar committees and executive notifications. Gangtok expanded as the administrative and trade center of the erstwhile Kingdom of Sikkim, necessitating structured management of roads, commercial stalls, drainage, hygiene and building construction.

### The Gangtok Municipal Corporation Act, 1975

Following Sikkim's integration into the Indian Union, the State Legislative Assembly enacted the **Gangtok Municipality, Corporation Act, 1975** (Act No. 4 of 1975). The statute established:

* A dedicated municipal governing authority for the capital.
* Public health and sanitation regulations.
* Authority to levy local rates, stall rents and municipal taxes.
* Frameworks for regulating building construction and commercial activities.

### The Sikkim Municipalities Act, 1995

To incorporate changes in town planning and expand administration beyond Gangtok, Sikkim enacted the Sikkim Municipalities Act, 1995. While it introduced updated legal definitions, it was subsequently replaced to bring state law into full alignment with the structural requirements of the 74th Constitutional Amendment.

### The Sikkim Municipalities Act, 2007

The **Sikkim Municipalities Act, 2007** (Act No. 5 of 2007) is the principal statute governing urban local authorities across Sikkim. It applies to all declared municipal jurisdictions except military cantonment areas.

The Act establishes:

* A three-tier classification of municipal authorities.
* Ward delimitation and electoral governance under the State Election Commission.
* The appointment of executive heads (Municipal Commissioner and Executive Officers).
* Constitution of a dedicated Municipal Fund, distinct budget procedures and revenue mechanisms.
* Provisions for financial accounts, internal audit, statutory audit and legislative reporting.
* Standing committees, subject committees and administrative supervisory powers of the State.

Under Section 3 and Section 7 of the Sikkim Municipalities Act, 2007, urban bodies are categorized based on population criteria, commercial activity and population density.

### 1. Municipal Corporation

Established for larger urban centers with a population of 70,000 or more.

* **Entity:** Gangtok Municipal Corporation (GMC).
* **Structure:** Headed by a Mayor, Deputy Mayor, an elected Council and a state-appointed Municipal Commissioner as executive authority.

### 2. Municipal Council

Established for medium-sized urban areas with populations between 10,000 and 70,000.

* **Entity:** Namchi Municipal Council.
* **Structure:** Headed by a Chairperson, Vice-Chairperson, Ward Councillors and a Municipal Executive Officer.

### 3. Nagar Panchayat

Established for transitional settlements evolving from rural to urban characteristics with populations under 10,000.

* **Entities:** Singtam, Pakyong, Rangpo, Gyalshing, Naya Bazar–Jorethang, Soreng and Mangan.
* **Structure:** Headed by a President, Vice-President, Ward Councillors and a Municipal Executive Officer.

### Historical Comparison of Urban Local Bodies

The structure of ULBs in Sikkim has transitioned over the past decade through administrative reorganization and district division:

| Category | March 2016 Structure | 2026 Election Cycle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Municipal Corporation | 1 (Gangtok) | 1 (Gangtok) |
| Municipal Council | 3 (Namchi, Jorethang, Geyzing) | 1 (Namchi) |
| Nagar Panchayat | 3 (Mangan, Singtam, Rangpo) | 7 (Singtam, Pakyong, Rangpo, Gyalshing, Naya Bazar–Jorethang, Soreng, Mangan) |
| **Total ULBs** | **7** | **9** |

As of March 2016, Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) reports recorded seven functional ULBs. With the administrative creation of Pakyong and Soreng districts and regional reclassifications, the municipal structure expanded to nine ULBs covering 63 wards by 2026.

## 5. Administrative and Organizational Structure

A municipality operates through two interdependent arms: an elected representative wing and an executive administrative wing.

```
          ┌──────────────────────────────────┐
          │      Elected Representative      │
          │               Wing               │
          └─────────────────┬────────────────┘
                            │ Policy and Approvals
                            ▼
          ┌──────────────────────────────────┐
          │     Executive Administrative     │
          │               Wing               │
          └─────────────────┬────────────────┘
                            │
       ┌────────────────────┼────────────────────┐
       ▼                    ▼                    ▼
Technical and        Sanitation and        Finance, Revenue
Engineering             Public Health         and Accounts

```

### Elected Wing

* **Presiding Executives:** Mayor in a Corporation; Chairperson in a Council; President in a Nagar Panchayat. Presides over council meetings, guides municipal policy, reviews development projects and oversees committee work.
* **Ward Councillors:** Directly elected by adult franchise from geographically delimited wards. They represent citizen grievances, deliberate on municipal by-laws, approve annual budget estimates and monitor ward-level works.

### Administrative Wing

* **Executive Authority:** The Municipal Commissioner serves as the executive head in the Municipal Corporation, while Municipal Executive Officers head Municipal Councils and Nagar Panchayats.
* **Key Responsibilities:** Execution of resolutions passed by the elected council; custody of municipal records and properties; supervision of personnel; procurement and contract management; preparation of annual accounts; and implementation of government-sanctioned schemes.
* **Specialized Units:** Subordinate engineering divisions, sanitation inspection cadres, tax collectors and accounting personnel.

## 6. Functional Operations

### Solid Waste Management

Solid waste management represents the largest operational commitment for Sikkim's municipalities. Under the Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016 and state directives, municipalities manage the end-to-end waste chain:

$$\text{Collection Coverage} = \frac{\text{Households Served}}{\text{Total Households}} \times 100$$

$$\text{Segregation Rate} = \frac{\text{Quantity of Segregated Waste Collected}}{\text{Total Waste Collected}} \times 100$$

$$\text{Processing Rate} = \frac{\text{Quantity of Waste Processed}}{\text{Total Waste Collected}} \times 100$$

Where:

* $\text{Collection Coverage}$ measures the percentage of households receiving regular collection services.
* $\text{Segregation Rate}$ reflects compliance with source-segregation standards (wet, dry and domestic hazardous waste).
* $\text{Processing Rate}$ measures the portion of waste handled via composting, material recovery facilities (MRFs) or recycling rather than open dumping.

### Civic Infrastructure and Public Amenities

* **Urban Transport and Road Networks:** Construction and maintenance of municipal feeder roads, pedestrian footpaths, stepped pathways in hilly terrain, stormwater drainage channels and street revetment structures.
* **Public Lighting:** Installation, operation and energy audit of public streetlighting networks, including conversion to LED fixtures.
* **Civic Utilities:** Operation of paid parking complexes, transport transit shelters, public conveniences and municipal market sheds.

### Regulatory and Civil Administration

* **Building Regulation:** Scrutiny of building plans, enforcement of structural safety, monitoring hill-slope stability requirements, issue of construction permits and occupancy certificates under Sikkim building bye-laws.
* **Trade Licensing:** Registration, annual licensing and fee collection for commercial establishments, wholesale outlets and hotels.
* **Vital Statistics:** Statutory registration of births and deaths and issuance of civil certificates under the Registration of Births and Deaths Act, 1969.

## 7. Municipal Finance and Revenue Administration

Urban local bodies in Sikkim maintain a distinct **Municipal Fund** credited with receipts from own-source revenue, assigned revenues, and fiscal transfers from higher tiers of government.

```
                      MUNICIPAL RECEIPTS
                              │
            ┌─────────────────┴─────────────────┐
            ▼                                   ▼
    Own-Source Revenue                  Intergovernmental
          (OSR)                             Transfers
     ┌──────┴──────┐                     ┌──────┴──────┐
     ▼             ▼                     ▼             ▼
  Tax Revenue   Non-Tax Revenue      Grants-in-Aid  Finance Commission
(Property Tax)  (Trade Licences,     (State/Central     Devolution
                 Fees, Charges)         Schemes)

```

### Own-Source Revenue (OSR)

Own-source revenue comprises tax and non-tax streams determined by the municipality within statutory ceilings:

* **Property and Holding Taxes:** Levied on land and buildings within municipal jurisdictions.
* **Licence Fees:** Annual fees from trade licences, vehicle registrations and commercial operations.
* **User Charges:** Sanitation service fees, parking collections, water maintenance cess and septic cleaning fees.
* **Commercial Rents:** Lease and rent receipts from municipal stalls, shopping complexes and commercial properties.
* **Regulatory Fees:** Building plan approval fees, developmental scrutiny charges, fines and late penalties.

### Intergovernmental Transfers

* **State Finance Commission (SFC):** Recommends principles governing the devolution of net state tax proceeds and grants-in-aid to urban bodies pursuant to Article 243Y of the Constitution.
* **Central Finance Commission (CFC):** Direct tied and untied grants aimed at supporting basic urban civic services, primarily sanitation, solid waste management and drinking water supply.
* **Programmatic Grants:** Central and State assistance under urban development initiatives (such as Atal Mission for Rejuvenation and Urban Transformation [AMRUT], Swachh Bharat Mission–Urban [SBM-U], and the National Urban Livelihoods Mission [DAY-NULM]).

In 2015–16, the seven ULBs received an aggregate of ₹1,230.71 lakh in Central and State grants, expending ₹825.49 lakh and carrying an unspent closing balance of ₹405.22 lakh.

## 8. Accounts, Audit and Accountability

### Accounting Standards and SULBAM

Under Section 56 and Section 57 of the Sikkim Municipalities Act, 2007, municipalities must maintain accounts and prepare annual financial statements.

* The Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs published the National Municipal Accounts Manual (NMAM) to encourage accrual-based double-entry accounting.
* The state drafted the **Sikkim Urban Local Bodies Accounting Manual (SULBAM)** to govern local ledger systems and cashbook reconciliations.
* Required accounting records include demand and collection registers, fixed asset registers, deposit work ledgers and monthly bank reconciliation statements.

### The Audit Framework

Sikkim employs a multi-tiered public sector audit architecture for urban local bodies:

```
            MUNICIPAL AUDIT ARCHITECTURE
                         │
        ┌────────────────┴────────────────┐
        ▼                                 ▼
   State Audit                       CAG Oversight
(Directorate of Local            (Technical Guidance
  Fund Audit - DLFA)              and Support - TGS)
        │                                 │
        ▼                                 ▼
Statutory Financial and           Compliance Audits and
Transaction Audits                Annual Technical Reports

```

1. **Directorate of Local Fund Audit (DLFA):** Established under the Sikkim Local Fund Audit Act, 2012, the DLFA serves as the primary statutory external auditor for local funds in the state.
2. **Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG):** Entrusted with the audit of ULBs under Section 20(1) of the CAG's (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971, following the state's adoption of the Technical Guidance and Support (TGS) framework in June 2011.
3. **Audit Procedures:** Reviews verify procurement propriety, the physical existence of assets, validity of Utilisation Certificates (UCs), adherence to delegated financial limits and revenue recovery performance.

### The 3E Audit Perspective

Performance reviews evaluate municipal operations against three fundamental parameters:

* **Economy:** Were physical inputs, vehicles and construction materials acquired at the lowest price consistent with required technical standards?
$$\text{Procurement Variance} = \text{Actual Unit Cost} - \text{Admissible Market Benchmark}$$


* **Efficiency:** Were human, financial and mechanical resources deployed to achieve maximum service output without operational idling?
* **Effectiveness:** Did project expenditures achieve the targeted social and public health outcomes, such as reduced waterborne illnesses, elimination of road bottlenecks or full solid waste containment?

## 10. Operational Challenges and Systemic Deficits

### Incomplete Functional Devolution

Although the Twelfth Schedule defines 18 civic subjects, early CAG institutional reviews indicated that only three functional areas had been transferred to Sikkim's ULBs with operational capacity:

1. Public health, sanitation and solid waste management.
2. Urban poverty alleviation.
3. Public amenities (streetlighting, parking lots and public conveniences).

Key sectors such as water supply, urban town planning, building fire safety and road construction remain divided among line departments—including the Urban Development Department (UDD) and the Public Health Engineering Department (PHED). This division frequently limits municipal autonomy.

### Fiscal Vulnerabilities and Revenue Gaps

Municipalities continue to experience structural financial deficits:

* **Low Own-Source Mobilization:** Operating revenues rely heavily on tied programmatic grants from central and state governments.
* **Outdated Valuation Rolls:** Property tax assessments have lagged behind rapid commercial development, leaving commercial properties unassessed or undertaxed.
* **Collection Losses:** Municipalities record low collection efficiency in water user charges, trade licence renewals and municipal stall lease arrears.

### Infrastructure Weaknesses and Mountain Geography

Urban centers in Sikkim face geographical constraints unique to the Eastern Himalayas:

* Unstable geological conditions and steep slopes limit options for solid waste landfill sites and material recycling plants.
* Heavy monsoon precipitation produces frequent flash floods, subsidence and structural damage to urban roads, requiring high annual maintenance expenditures.
* Seasonal tourist inflows expand municipal populations significantly, straining existing water delivery networks, traffic capacity and parking infrastructure.

### Accounting and Audit Deficiencies

Historical audit reviews by the DLFA and the CAG have highlighted administrative deficiencies across ULBs:

* **Maintenance of Records:** Delays in finalizing annual balance sheets and double-entry books under SULBAM frameworks.
* **Asset Inventories:** Absence of comprehensive fixed asset registers recording land parcels, buildings, road works and vehicles.
* **Utilisation Certificate Discrepancies:** Submitting final UCs to funding agencies before expenditure has been verified through site measurement and cashbook reconciliation.
* **Unsettled Objections:** Accumulation of outstanding audit paragraphs resulting from delayed replies to Inspection Reports.

## 11. Strategic Reform Priorities

To establish urban local bodies as self-sustaining institutions of local self-government, several administrative and financial reforms are needed:

### 1. Structural Devolution (3Fs Framework)

* The State Government should publish a functional devolution notification aligning the transfer of **Functions, Funds and Functionaries**.
* Clear administrative boundaries should prevent jurisdictional overlap between municipal engineering cells and state line departments.

### 2. Modernizing Municipal Revenue

* Adopt Geographic Information System (GIS) mapping for property tax assessment to eliminate unassessed commercial holdings.
* Introduce automated online platforms for trade licence issuance, building permits and monthly user fees.
* Periodically adjust parking and user charge rates to recover actual operation and maintenance expenses.

### 3. Financial Discipline and Digital Record Management

* Fully implement the SULBAM double-entry accounting platform across all nine municipal bodies.
* Enforce monthly bank reconciliation before issuing fund disbursements.
* Compile and maintain fixed-asset registers, supported by annual physical verification.
* Establish an active Municipal Accounts Committee (MAC) within each council to review DLFA and CAG inspection reports.

### 4. Regional Technical Cells

* Establish shared technical divisions at the district level—employing civil engineers, town planners and environmental specialists—to assist smaller Nagar Panchayats that cannot maintain dedicated technical cadres.
