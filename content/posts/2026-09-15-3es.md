# The 3Es in Audit: Economy, Efficiency and Effectiveness

The **3Es in audit** are:

1. **Economy** — obtaining resources at the lowest reasonable cost while maintaining suitable quality.
2. **Efficiency** — obtaining the maximum possible output from the resources used.
3. **Effectiveness** — achieving the intended objectives and results.

The 3Es form the foundation of **performance audit**. The Comptroller and Auditor General of India describes performance audit as an independent, objective and reliable examination of whether public-sector organisations, programmes, systems and activities operate according to the principles of economy, efficiency and effectiveness. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/en/page-performance-audit)

In simple language:

> Economy asks, “Did we buy inputs at a reasonable cost?”  
> Efficiency asks, “Did we use those inputs properly?”  
> Effectiveness asks, “Did we achieve the intended result?”

***

## 1. Why the 3Es Matter

Traditional financial audit mainly asks whether the accounts are correct.

Compliance audit mainly asks whether laws, rules and instructions were followed.

The 3Es ask a broader question:

> Did public money produce reasonable value for citizens?

A local body may spend money legally and record it correctly, but still fail the 3Es.

### Example

A municipality spends ₹10 lakh on a water-supply project.

- **Economy:** Was the material purchased at a reasonable price?
- **Efficiency:** Was the project completed using the planned quantity of labour, material and time?
- **Effectiveness:** Did the project actually provide reliable drinking water to the intended households?

The expenditure may be fully supported by vouchers, but if the water system does not work, the project may be ineffective.

***

## 2. Performance Audit and the 3Es

The 3Es are mainly examined through performance audit.

The CAG’s Regulations on Audit and Accounts state that performance audit examines whether government entities, institutions, operations, programmes, funds and activities operate according to economy, efficiency and effectiveness, and whether there is room for improvement. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/uploads/media/Book-Regulations-Audit-Accounts-2020-20200909192352.pdf)

Performance audit may examine:

- Inputs.
- Processes.
- Outputs.
- Outcomes.
- Impacts.
- Management systems.
- Internal controls.
- Public service delivery.
- Value for money.

### Simple explanation

Performance audit does not examine only the final accounts. It studies the complete chain from money and resources to results.

```text
Inputs → Activities → Outputs → Outcomes → Impact
```

### Example: rural road project

```text
Inputs:
₹50 lakh, cement, labour, machinery

Activities:
Tendering, construction and supervision

Outputs:
5 kilometres of road constructed

Outcomes:
Better transport and reduced travel time

Impact:
Improved access to markets, schools and health services
```

The 3Es can be applied at each stage.

# Part I: Economy

## 3. Meaning of Economy

Economy means obtaining resources at the lowest reasonable cost while maintaining the required quality and quantity.

The CAG’s Performance Auditing Guidelines explain economy as minimising the cost of resources by acquiring them:

- At the proper time.
- In the appropriate quantity.
- At the appropriate quality.
- At the best or most reasonable price. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/uploads/guidelines/PA-Guidelines2014-05-updated-062f4aa3d0af371-98564945.pdf)

### Simple definition

> Economy means “buying the right input at the right quality, at the right time and at the right price.”

Economy mainly concerns the cost of **inputs**.

### Common inputs

- Human resources.
- Materials.
- Equipment.
- Vehicles.
- Buildings.
- Information technology.
- Consultants.
- Contractors.
- Electricity.
- Water.
- Office supplies.

***

## 4. What Economy Does Not Mean

Economy does not simply mean buying the cheapest item.

The cheapest item may be poor quality, unsuitable or unreliable.

### Example

A local body buys two types of streetlights:

- Brand A: ₹4,000 each, expected life 2 years.
- Brand B: ₹5,000 each, expected life 8 years.

Brand A appears cheaper, but Brand B may be more economical over its useful life.

The auditor should consider:

- Purchase price.
- Quality.
- Useful life.
- Maintenance cost.
- Energy consumption.
- Replacement cost.
- Warranty.
- Availability of spare parts.

### Life-cycle cost

A useful calculation is:

[Life-Cycle Cost=Purchase Cost+Operating Cost+Maintenance Cost+Disposal Cost]

The cheapest purchase may not produce the lowest life-cycle cost.

***

## 5. Economy in Local Fund Audit

Economy can be examined in the following areas:

### Procurement

- Was competitive procurement used?
- Were specifications reasonable?
- Were quotations comparable?
- Was the selected price reasonable?
- Was the purchase split to avoid tender requirements?
- Was the quantity based on actual need?
- Was the item bought at the appropriate time?

### Works

- Was the estimate based on realistic rates?
- Were materials purchased at approved rates?
- Was excess material procured?
- Were transport costs reasonable?
- Were standard schedules of rates followed?
- Were rates compared with similar works?

### Staffing

- Were temporary workers engaged only when necessary?
- Was overtime justified?
- Were vacant posts causing avoidable expenditure?
- Were consultants used when departmental expertise was available?

### Vehicles

- Was vehicle hiring economical?
- Were journeys necessary?
- Was fuel consumption reasonable?
- Was maintenance cost excessive?

### Technology

- Was software procured after assessing actual needs?
- Were licences purchased but not used?
- Was hardware compatible with existing systems?
- Were maintenance and support costs considered?

***

## 6. Economy Audit Questions

The auditor may ask:

1. What resources were required?
2. How was the requirement estimated?
3. Was the purchase necessary?
4. Was the quantity reasonable?
5. Was the timing appropriate?
6. Were specifications suitable?
7. Was competition adequate?
8. Was the price compared with market rates?
9. Was the quality satisfactory?
10. Were total life-cycle costs considered?
11. Were there cheaper alternatives of equivalent quality?
12. Did delay increase the cost?
13. Was emergency procurement genuinely necessary?
14. Were purchases made in a transparent manner?

***

## 7. Economy Example: Procurement of Computers

Suppose a Panchayat buys 10 computers.

| Particular | Economy question |
|---|---|
| Need | Were 10 computers actually required? |
| Specification | Was the specification appropriate or unnecessarily expensive? |
| Price | Was the price compared with market rates? |
| Competition | Were adequate quotations obtained? |
| Quantity | Were all 10 computers used? |
| Timing | Were they purchased before the programme began? |
| Quality | Were the computers fit for the intended work? |
| Maintenance | Were warranty and maintenance costs considered? |

### Possible audit finding

The Panchayat purchased 10 computers at ₹70,000 each, although similar computers were available at ₹50,000 each with equivalent specifications. Four computers remained unused for more than one year.

### Financial effect

\[
10 \times (₹70,000 - ₹50,000)
=
₹2,00,000
\]

This may indicate:

- Uneconomical procurement.
- Inadequate market comparison.
- Weak needs assessment.
- Poor asset-utilisation planning.

***

## 8. Economy Indicators

Useful economy indicators include:

### Purchase price variance
$$
\[
\text{Purchase Price Variance}
=
\text{Actual Price}
-
\text{Benchmark Price}
\]
$$
### Cost per unit
$$
\[
\text{Cost per Unit}
=
\frac{\text{Total Cost}}{\text{Quantity Purchased}}
\]
$$
### Procurement saving
$$
\[
\text{Procurement Saving}
=
\text{Estimated Cost}
-
\text{Actual Contract Cost}
\]
$$
The auditor must interpret these figures carefully. A lower contract cost is not automatically an economy achievement if quality is poor or the work is incomplete.

### Other indicators

- Percentage of purchases made through competition.
- Percentage of emergency purchases.
- Average cost per item.
- Cost compared with approved schedule of rates.
- Cost compared with similar local bodies.
- Percentage of unused materials.
- Cost of avoidable storage.
- Cost of idle equipment.
- Cost of delayed procurement.

***

# Part II: Efficiency

## 9. Meaning of Efficiency

Efficiency means obtaining the maximum output from the resources used, or obtaining a given output with the minimum necessary resources.

The CAG explains efficiency as the relationship between resources employed and outputs delivered in terms of:

- Quantity.
- Quality.
- Timing. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/uploads/media/Book-Regulations-Audit-Accounts-2020-20200909192352.pdf)

### Simple definition

> Efficiency means “doing the work properly with minimum waste of time, money, labour and materials.”

Efficiency focuses on the relationship between **inputs and outputs**.
$$
\[
\text{Efficiency}
=
\frac{\text{Outputs}}{\text{Inputs}}
\]
$$
***

## 10. Inputs, Outputs and Efficiency

### Inputs

Inputs are resources used to perform an activity.

Examples:

- Money.
- Staff.
- Materials.
- Equipment.
- Time.
- Vehicles.
- Office space.
- Data.

### Outputs

Outputs are immediate products or services produced.

Examples:

- Kilometres of road constructed.
- Number of houses completed.
- Number of tax assessments issued.
- Number of beneficiaries registered.
- Number of water connections provided.
- Number of audit reports issued.

### Efficiency relationship

If two local bodies produce the same output, the one using fewer resources is generally more efficient, provided quality and timing are comparable.

***

## 11. Efficiency Versus Economy

Economy examines the cost of inputs.

Efficiency examines how well those inputs are converted into outputs.

### Example

A municipality buys cement economically at ₹400 per bag.

But if 20 per cent of the cement is wasted, the activity may not be efficient.

| Question | 3E |
|---|---|
| Was cement bought at a reasonable price? | Economy |
| Was the cement used without unnecessary waste? | Efficiency |
| Did the completed work solve the identified problem? | Effectiveness |

***

## 12. Efficiency in Local Fund Audit

Efficiency can be examined in:

### Service delivery

- Time taken to issue certificates.
- Cost per household served.
- Staff productivity.
- Number of applications processed.
- Percentage of complaints resolved on time.

### Works

- Cost per kilometre.
- Time taken to complete.
- Labour productivity.
- Material utilisation.
- Equipment utilisation.
- Percentage of idle time.
- Percentage of incomplete works.

### Revenue administration

- Cost of collection.
- Collection per revenue staff member.
- Percentage of demand collected.
- Time taken to update assessments.
- Number of properties surveyed per team.

### Audit administration

- Number of local bodies audited.
- Audit days per institution.
- Cost per audit.
- Reports issued within time.
- Number of objections followed up.
- Average time taken to settle objections.

***

## 13. Efficiency Audit Questions

The auditor may ask:

1. Were resources used fully?
2. Was there unnecessary idle time?
3. Was the process properly organised?
4. Were activities delayed?
5. Could the same output have been produced with fewer resources?
6. Were staff deployed according to workload?
7. Were materials wasted?
8. Were machines and vehicles utilised?
9. Were services delivered within the prescribed time?
10. Were there avoidable bottlenecks?
11. Did poor planning increase cost?
12. Were similar institutions performing better?
13. Were internal controls causing unnecessary delay?
14. Was technology used effectively?

***

## 14. Efficiency Example: Road Construction

A local body completes 4 kilometres of road using:

- ₹40 lakh.
- 120 labour-months.
- 8 months.
- 1,000 tonnes of material.

Another local body completes 4 kilometres of similar quality road using:

- ₹36 lakh.
- 100 labour-months.
- 6 months.
- 900 tonnes of material.

### Indicators

#### Cost per kilometre

First local body:

$$
\[
\frac{₹40,00,000}{4}
=
₹10,00,000 \text{ per km}
\]
$$

Second local body:

$$
\[
\frac{₹36,00,000}{4}
=
₹9,00,000 \text{ per km}
\]
$$
#### Time per kilometre

First local body:
$$
\[
\frac{8}{4}
=
2 \text{ months per km}
\]
$$
Second local body:
$$
\[
\frac{6}{4}
=
1.5 \text{ months per km}
\]
$$
The second local body appears more efficient, but the auditor must also compare:

- Road quality.
- Terrain.
- Weather.
- Labour rates.
- Material availability.
- Design specifications.
- Number of beneficiaries.

A comparison without considering these factors may be misleading.

***

## 15. Efficiency and Benchmarking

Efficiency can be assessed by comparing performance with:

- Previous years.
- Similar local bodies.
- Approved standards.
- Departmental norms.
- Schedule of rates.
- Service-level benchmarks.
- Best practices.
- Project estimates.
- Contract conditions.

### Important caution

A benchmark should be comparable.

For example, comparing a hill district road with a plain-area road may be inappropriate unless adjustments are made for:

- Terrain.
- Transport distance.
- Climate.
- Labour rates.
- Material availability.
- Population density.

***

## 16. Efficiency Indicators

### Output per employee
$$
\[
\text{Output per Employee}
=
\frac{\text{Total Output}}{\text{Number of Employees}}
\]
$$
### Cost per service unit
$$
\[
\text{Cost per Service Unit}
=
\frac{\text{Total Expenditure}}{\text{Units of Service Delivered}}
\]
$$
### Time efficiency
$$
\[
\text{Average Processing Time}
=
\frac{\text{Total Processing Time}}{\text{Number of Cases}}
\]
$$
### Utilisation rate
$$
\[
\text{Utilisation Rate}
=
\frac{\text{Actual Usage}}{\text{Available Capacity}}
\times 100
\]
$$
### Completion rate
$$
\[
\text{Completion Rate}
=
\frac{\text{Completed Activities}}{\text{Planned Activities}}
\times 100
\]
$$
### Material wastage
$$
\[
\text{Wastage Rate}
=
\frac{\text{Material Issued} - \text{Material Used}}{\text{Material Issued}}
\times 100
\]
$$
### Audit productivity
$$
\[
\text{Audit Productivity}
=
\frac{\text{Audit Units Completed}}{\text{Audit Staff-Days}}
\]
$$
Productivity should not be judged only by the number of audits. Quality, complexity and significance must also be considered.

# Part III: Effectiveness

## 17. Meaning of Effectiveness

Effectiveness means achieving the objectives set for a programme, activity or organisation.

The CAG describes effectiveness as the extent to which objectives are achieved and the relationship between intended impact and actual impact. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/en/page-regulations-audit-accounts-2007)

### Simple definition

> Effectiveness means “Did the programme achieve what it was supposed to achieve?”

Effectiveness focuses on:

- Outputs.
- Outcomes.
- Impacts.
- Benefits to citizens.
- Achievement of policy objectives.

***

## 18. Outputs, Outcomes and Impacts

These terms should be distinguished carefully.

### Output

The immediate product of an activity.

Examples:

- 100 toilets constructed.
- 500 households connected to water supply.
- 10 kilometres of road completed.
- 1,000 tax notices issued.

### Outcome

The short- or medium-term result of the output.

Examples:

- More households use toilets.
- Water availability improves.
- Travel time decreases.
- Tax collection increases.

### Impact

The broader long-term effect.

Examples:

- Reduced waterborne disease.
- Improved household income.
- Better school attendance.
- Improved public health.
- Stronger local revenue base.

```text
Input → Activity → Output → Outcome → Impact
```

### Example

A municipality installs 50 streetlights.

- **Output:** 50 streetlights installed.
- **Outcome:** Roads become safer and better illuminated.
- **Impact:** Night-time accidents and crime reduce, and public movement improves.

If the lights are installed but never connected to electricity, the output exists but the intended outcome may not be achieved.

***

## 19. Effectiveness in Local Fund Audit

Effectiveness may be assessed in:

### Water supply

- Households receiving water.
- Hours of supply.
- Water quality.
- Reliability.
- Reduction in water shortages.
- Citizen satisfaction.

### Sanitation

- Toilets constructed and usable.
- Waste collected.
- Waste treated.
- Open dumping reduced.
- Drainage improved.
- Disease incidence reduced.

### Roads

- Road completed.
- Road usable in all seasons.
- Travel time reduced.
- Access to markets and schools improved.
- Maintenance requirements reduced.

### Revenue

- Tax base expanded.
- Collection improved.
- Arrears reduced.
- Financial sustainability strengthened.

### Audit

- Audit objections corrected.
- Repeat irregularities reduced.
- Recovery completed.
- Internal controls improved.
- Accounts prepared on time.

***

## 20. Effectiveness Audit Questions

The auditor may ask:

1. What was the objective of the programme?
2. Was the objective clearly defined?
3. Were the target beneficiaries correctly identified?
4. Were adequate resources provided?
5. Were activities implemented as planned?
6. Were outputs produced?
7. Were outputs of acceptable quality?
8. Were outputs used by beneficiaries?
9. Were the intended outcomes achieved?
10. What evidence proves the result?
11. Were there external factors affecting performance?
12. Did the programme overlap with another programme?
13. Were benefits equitably distributed?
14. Was monitoring reliable?
15. What changes are required?

***

## 21. Effectiveness Example: Drinking Water Scheme

Suppose a Panchayat receives ₹25 lakh to provide drinking water to 200 households.

### Audit examination

| Stage | Question |
|---|---|
| Objective | Was the aim to provide regular drinking water to 200 households? |
| Inputs | Was ₹25 lakh received and available? |
| Activity | Was the pipeline and storage tank constructed? |
| Output | Were 200 household connections provided? |
| Quality | Was the water tested and safe? |
| Outcome | Did households receive water regularly? |
| Impact | Did dependence on distant water sources reduce? |

### Possible result

- ₹25 lakh spent.
- Tank constructed.
- 200 connections shown in records.
- Only 80 households receive water.
- Water supply operates for two hours once every five days.

The project may be:

- Economical in procurement.
- Partly efficient in construction.
- Ineffective in achieving reliable water supply.

***

## 22. Effectiveness Indicators

### Target achievement
$$
\[
\text{Target Achievement}
=
\frac{\text{Actual Achievement}}{\text{Planned Target}}
\times 100
\]
$$
### Coverage
$$
\[
\text{Coverage Rate}
=
\frac{\text{Beneficiaries Served}}{\text{Eligible Beneficiaries}}
\times 100
\]
$$
### Outcome achievement
$$
\[
\text{Outcome Achievement}
=
\frac{\text{Actual Outcome}}{\text{Desired Outcome}}
\times 100
\]
$$
### Service availability
$$
\[
\text{Service Availability}
=
\frac{\text{Actual Service Hours}}{\text{Planned Service Hours}}
\times 100
\]
$$
### Asset utilisation
$$
\[
\text{Asset Utilisation}
=
\frac{\text{Assets Functioning}}{\text{Assets Created}}
\times 100
\]
$$
### Beneficiary satisfaction
$$
\[
\text{Satisfaction Rate}
=
\frac{\text{Satisfied Beneficiaries}}{\text{Beneficiaries Surveyed}}
\times 100
\]
$$
Quantitative figures must be supported by reliable evidence.

# Part IV: Relationship Among the 3Es

## 23. How Economy, Efficiency and Effectiveness Connect

The 3Es are separate concepts, but they are connected.

```text
Economy → Efficient use → Effective result
```

A programme may achieve one E but fail another.

### Example 1: Economical but ineffective

A Panchayat buys low-cost water pumps, but the pumps are unsuitable and fail within three months.

- Economy: Possibly achieved at purchase stage.
- Efficiency: Weak because resources were not converted into reliable output.
- Effectiveness: Not achieved because water supply failed.

### Example 2: Effective but not economical

A road is completed and provides good public benefit, but the procurement price was far above reasonable market rates.

- Economy: Not achieved.
- Efficiency: May be weak.
- Effectiveness: Achieved.

### Example 3: Efficient but ineffective

A local body processes 1,000 applications quickly, but the applications are incorrectly assessed and benefits go to ineligible persons.

- Efficiency: High in processing speed.
- Effectiveness: Low because the intended beneficiaries were not correctly served.

### Example 4: All three achieved

A municipality buys suitable streetlights at a competitive price, installs them within schedule and improves public safety.

- Economy: Reasonable cost.
- Efficiency: Timely and low-waste installation.
- Effectiveness: Intended public-safety result achieved.

***

## 24. The 3Es Compared

| Dimension | Economy | Efficiency | Effectiveness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main question | Did we obtain inputs at a reasonable cost? | Did we use resources productively? | Did we achieve the intended objective? |
| Focus | Inputs | Inputs and outputs | Outputs, outcomes and impacts |
| Concern | Price, quality and timing | Productivity, waste and timeliness | Goal achievement and public benefit |
| Typical measure | Cost per input | Cost per output | Target or outcome achievement |
| Example | Buy cement at a reasonable rate | Use cement without waste | Build a durable road serving citizens |
| Failure example | Overpriced purchase | Excessive delay | Road does not improve access |
| Main evidence | Quotations, tenders, rates | Work records, time sheets, output data | Outcome data, surveys, field inspection |

# Part V: 3Es and Other Types of Audit

## 25. 3Es in Financial Audit

Financial audit checks whether the financial statements and accounts are reliable.

The 3Es may arise when the auditor examines:

- Unusual expenditure.
- Excessive cost.
- Unutilised assets.
- Wasteful expenditure.
- Large outstanding advances.
- Unnecessary procurement.
- Idle investments.

### Example

If a local body purchased equipment that was never used, the financial statements may still record the purchase correctly. Financial audit may confirm the accounting, while performance audit asks whether the purchase was economical, efficient and effective.

***

## 26. 3Es in Compliance Audit

Compliance audit checks whether transactions comply with laws, rules and instructions.

A violation may also indicate failure of one or more Es.

### Example

A local body bypasses tender procedure:

- Compliance issue: Procurement rules were violated.
- Economy issue: The price may not have been competitive.
- Efficiency issue: Procurement may have been delayed or poorly managed.
- Effectiveness issue: The purchased item may not meet the programme objective.

The auditor should not automatically assume that every rule violation caused financial loss, but the risk should be examined.

***

## 27. 3Es in Local Fund Audit

Local fund audit traditionally focuses on:

- Receipts.
- Payments.
- Accounts.
- Cash.
- Stores.
- Assets.
- Compliance.
- Losses.
- Misappropriation.

However, local fund audit can also include 3E analysis.

### Economy

- Was the local body purchasing economically?
- Were funds wasted?
- Were rates reasonable?

### Efficiency

- Were local services delivered with reasonable resources?
- Were projects delayed?
- Were assets idle?

### Effectiveness

- Did projects benefit citizens?
- Were intended outputs and outcomes achieved?
- Did repeated audit findings lead to improvement?

***

## 28. 3Es and DLFA

The DLFA can incorporate the 3Es into local fund audit by:

- Selecting high-value and high-impact programmes.
- Reviewing major works.
- Comparing costs among similar local bodies.
- Examining unused assets.
- Reviewing delayed and incomplete projects.
- Checking service outcomes.
- Conducting beneficiary verification.
- Analysing repeated failure of schemes.
- Assessing revenue collection efficiency.
- Reporting systemic causes.

The Sikkim Local Fund Audit Act, 2012 gives the Director power to conduct annual audit, concurrent audit, special audit, cash verification and stock inspection. These powers can support 3E-focused audits where permitted by the statutory mandate. [sikkim.gov](https://www.sikkim.gov.in/uploads/Gazette/2012_THE_SIKKIM_LOCAL_FUND_AUDIT_ACT_20210908.pdf)

# Part VI: Planning a 3E Audit

## 29. Select the Audit Topic

A good topic should be:

- Financially significant.
- Socially important.
- High risk.
- Auditable.
- Relevant to citizens.
- Capable of producing improvement.

### Possible local-body topics

- Drinking-water supply.
- Solid-waste management.
- Property-tax collection.
- Rural-road construction.
- Municipal markets.
- Streetlighting.
- Sanitation.
- Finance Commission grants.
- Public works.
- Local-body digital systems.
- Audit-objection settlement.

The CAG states that performance topics should be selected through strategic planning, risk analysis and study of potential problems. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/en/page-performance-audit)

***

## 30. Define the Audit Objective

The audit objective should be specific.

### Weak objective

> To examine the performance of the water-supply scheme.

### Better objective

> To assess whether the rural water-supply scheme was implemented economically and efficiently and whether it achieved the planned coverage and reliability of drinking-water services during 2024–25 to 2025–26.

A good objective identifies:

- Programme.
- Period.
- Entities.
- 3Es.
- Intended result.

***

## 31. Formulate Audit Questions

Audit questions break the objective into testable parts.

### Example: waste-management audit

#### Economy

- Were vehicles, bins and equipment procured at reasonable cost?
- Were specifications and quantities based on actual requirements?

#### Efficiency

- Were collection vehicles and workers properly utilised?
- Was waste collected according to schedule?
- Was fuel consumption reasonable?

#### Effectiveness

- Was waste actually collected from intended areas?
- Was waste segregated and processed?
- Did open dumping reduce?

***

## 32. Establish Audit Criteria

Audit criteria are the standards against which performance is compared.

Possible criteria include:

- Acts and rules.
- Government orders.
- Scheme guidelines.
- Approved budgets.
- Contracts.
- Technical standards.
- Service-level benchmarks.
- Approved project reports.
- Municipal resolutions.
- Panchayat development plans.
- Departmental norms.
- Good practices.
- Previous performance.

The CAG’s Performance Auditing Guidelines state that criteria should be reasonable, audit-specific, relevant, understandable, complete, reliable and objective. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/uploads/guidelines/PA-Guidelines2014-05-updated-062f4aa3d0af371-98564945.pdf)

### Criteria example

| Audit question | Possible criterion |
|---|---|
| Was road work completed on time? | Contract completion period |
| Was material purchased economically? | Approved rates and market comparison |
| Were beneficiaries covered? | Scheme target and eligibility list |
| Was waste collected daily? | Municipal service standard |
| Were assets used? | Approved utilisation plan |

***

## 33. Develop an Audit Design Matrix

A design matrix links the audit objective to evidence and conclusions.

| Audit question | Criteria | Evidence | Method | Expected conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Was procurement economical? | Approved rate and procurement rules | Tender file, quotations, invoices | Document examination | Whether price was reasonable |
| Was the project completed efficiently? | Work schedule and estimate | MB, progress reports, site inspection | Comparison and physical verification | Whether time and resources were used well |
| Did citizens benefit? | Coverage and service target | Beneficiary list, survey, field visit | Data analysis and interviews | Whether outcome was achieved |

This matrix prevents the audit from becoming a collection of unrelated observations.

***

## 34. Select Audit Evidence

A 3E audit may require:

- Administrative records.
- Financial statements.
- Budget documents.
- Tender files.
- Contracts.
- Measurement books.
- Stock registers.
- Asset registers.
- Progress reports.
- Management information systems.
- Photographs.
- Geospatial data.
- Beneficiary records.
- Field inspection.
- Interviews.
- Surveys.
- Complaint registers.
- Bank records.
- Comparative data.

Evidence should be:

- Sufficient.
- Appropriate.
- Reliable.
- Relevant.
- Verifiable.

***

## 35. Use Sampling Carefully

The auditor may examine all transactions or select a sample.

### Sample selection should consider

- Value.
- Risk.
- Frequency.
- Unusual nature.
- Geographic distribution.
- Time period.
- Entity size.
- Previous irregularities.

### Use a mixed sample

A useful sample may include:

- All high-value transactions.
- All unusual transactions.
- Random sample of routine transactions.
- Transactions from different months.
- Transactions involving different suppliers.
- Transactions from different locations.

### Example

If a local body makes 1,000 payments:

- Examine all 20 payments above ₹1 lakh.
- Examine all emergency purchases.
- Examine all payments to new suppliers.
- Select a random sample of routine payments.
- Select transactions from each quarter.

***

# Part VII: 3E Audit Findings

## 36. Structure of a 3E Finding

A strong performance audit finding generally includes:

1. Condition.
2. Criteria.
3. Cause.
4. Effect.
5. Evidence.
6. Management response.
7. Conclusion.
8. Recommendation.

### Example

#### Title

**Inefficient procurement and under-utilisation of waste-collection vehicles**

#### Condition

The municipality purchased five waste-collection vehicles at a total cost of ₹75 lakh. Two vehicles remained unused for more than 14 months because drivers were not appointed.

#### Criteria

Vehicles should be procured according to an approved operational plan and placed into service within a reasonable period.

#### Cause

Procurement was completed without ensuring availability of drivers and maintenance arrangements.

#### Effect

₹30 lakh worth of vehicles remained idle. The municipality also continued to hire private vehicles for waste collection.

#### 3E conclusion

- **Economy:** Procurement was not economical because the total operational cost was not considered.
- **Efficiency:** Resources were inefficiently used because vehicles remained idle.
- **Effectiveness:** Waste-collection coverage did not improve as planned.

#### Recommendation

The municipality should prepare a capacity plan before future procurement, deploy the vehicles immediately where justified and review responsibility for the idle period.

***

## 37. Findings Must Be Balanced

A performance audit report should not contain only criticism.

The CAG’s guidance expects reports to be:

- Complete.
- Accurate.
- Objective.
- Convincing.
- Clear.
- Concise.
- Constructive.
- Timely. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/en/page-regulations-audit-accounts-2007)

The report should consider:

- Management explanations.
- External factors.
- Positive achievements.
- Data limitations.
- Differences between local bodies.
- The role of other agencies.
- Whether the result can fairly be attributed to the programme.

***

## 38. Distinguish Failure From External Factors

Effectiveness may be affected by factors outside the control of the local body, such as:

- Floods.
- Landslides.
- Extreme weather.
- Sudden price increases.
- Land disputes.
- Court orders.
- Delayed release of funds.
- Changes in government policy.
- Shortage of technical staff.
- Delayed permissions.

The auditor should determine whether the local body:

- Identified the risk.
- Took reasonable action.
- Reported the problem.
- Revised the plan.
- Protected public funds.
- Sought timely assistance.

A project is not automatically ineffective merely because an external event caused delay.

# Part VIII: Improving 3E Performance

## 39. Improving Economy

Local bodies can improve economy by:

- Preparing realistic procurement plans.
- Aggregating common purchases where suitable.
- Using competitive procurement.
- Comparing market rates.
- Avoiding unnecessary specifications.
- Considering life-cycle costs.
- Buying according to actual demand.
- Reducing emergency procurement.
- Preventing overstocking.
- Reviewing supplier performance.
- Using rate contracts where appropriate.
- Monitoring transport and storage cost.

***

## 40. Improving Efficiency

Efficiency can be improved by:

- Simplifying administrative procedures.
- Assigning work according to skills.
- Reducing approval delays.
- Using technology.
- Tracking pending cases.
- Maintaining accurate work schedules.
- Monitoring staff attendance and output.
- Reducing material wastage.
- Using vehicles and machinery effectively.
- Coordinating departments.
- Reviewing idle assets.
- Comparing performance across local bodies.

***

## 41. Improving Effectiveness

Effectiveness can be improved by:

- Defining measurable objectives.
- Identifying actual beneficiaries.
- Linking budgets to outputs and outcomes.
- Monitoring service quality.
- Conducting field inspections.
- Obtaining beneficiary feedback.
- Correcting implementation weaknesses.
- Coordinating related schemes.
- Measuring actual results.
- Reviewing whether assets remain functional.
- Taking action on outcome failures.
- Publishing understandable performance information.

# Part X: 3Es in Sikkim and the DLFA

## 45. Application to Sikkim Local Bodies

The Sikkim Local Fund Audit Act, 2012 covers core urban and rural local authorities, including:

- Municipal Corporations.
- Municipalities.
- Gram Panchayat Units.
- Zilla Panchayats. [sikkim.gov](https://www.sikkim.gov.in/uploads/Gazette/2012_THE_SIKKIM_LOCAL_FUND_AUDIT_ACT_20210908.pdf)

The Act also permits annual audit, concurrent audit, special audit, examination of stock and stores, cash verification and demands for records.

These statutory powers can support examination of 3E issues such as:

- Whether local funds were spent economically.
- Whether local-body operations converted resources into outputs efficiently.
- Whether development activities achieved intended results.
- Whether cash, stores and assets were properly managed.
- Whether significant irregularities affected service delivery.

### Example: DLFA audit of a Panchayat road

#### Economy

- Were materials purchased at reasonable rates?
- Was the quantity based on the estimate?
- Were transport and storage costs reasonable?

#### Efficiency

- Was the road completed within the approved time?
- Was material wasted?
- Were labour and machinery properly utilised?
- Was payment proportionate to actual work?

#### Effectiveness

- Does the road connect the intended habitations?
- Is it usable during the rainy season?
- Has travel time reduced?
- Are schools, markets and health centres more accessible?

# Part XI: Common Mistakes in 3E Audit

## 47. Mistake 1: Treating Lowest Price as Economy

The lowest price may mean poor quality, short useful life or higher maintenance cost.

### Better approach

Compare total life-cycle cost and quality.

***

## 48. Mistake 2: Treating Expenditure as Output

Spending ₹1 crore does not prove that a programme achieved its objective.

### Better approach

Verify what was produced and whether it was used.

***

## 49. Mistake 3: Treating Output as Outcome

Constructing a building does not prove that services improved.

### Better approach

Check whether the building is functional, staffed and used by citizens.

***

## 50. Mistake 4: Ignoring Quality

A project completed on time may still be inefficient or ineffective if its quality is poor.

### Better approach

Verify quality through technical records, inspection and user experience.

***

## 51. Mistake 5: Comparing Incomparable Units

Different terrain, population, workload and input prices can affect performance.

### Better approach

Use comparable units or adjust for important differences.

***

## 52. Mistake 6: Blaming the Local Body for External Delays

Funding delays or natural disasters may affect performance.

### Better approach

Examine whether the local authority responded reasonably and protected public money.

***

## 53. Mistake 7: Making Recommendations Too General

“Take necessary action” is usually not a useful recommendation.

### Better recommendation

> The Executive Officer should prepare a quarterly project-monitoring statement showing planned cost, actual cost, planned completion date, actual progress, reasons for variation and corrective action. The statement should be reviewed by the competent committee every quarter.

***

## 54. Mistake 8: Ignoring Follow-Up

A report does not create improvement unless corrective action is checked.

The CAG’s guidance states that follow-up should examine whether the audited entity has addressed identified problems and remedied the underlying situation, not merely whether it has formally accepted a recommendation. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/en/page-performance-audit)

***

# Part XIV: Important Formulas

## 57. Economy Formulas

### Cost variance
$$
\[
\text{Cost Variance}
=
\text{Actual Cost}
-
\text{Standard or Benchmark Cost}
\]
$$
### Percentage cost variance
$$
\[
\text{Cost Variance Percentage}
=
\frac{\text{Actual Cost} - \text{Benchmark Cost}}
{\text{Benchmark Cost}}
\times 100
\]
$$
### Life-cycle cost
$$
\[
\text{Life-Cycle Cost}
=
\text{Purchase}
+
\text{Operation}
+
\text{Maintenance}
+
\text{Disposal}
\]
$$
***

## 58. Efficiency Formulas

### Output per unit of input
$$
\[
\text{Productivity}
=
\frac{\text{Output}}{\text{Input}}
\]
$$
### Unit cost
$$
\[
\text{Unit Cost}
=
\frac{\text{Total Input Cost}}{\text{Output}}
\]
$$
### Capacity utilisation
$$
\[
\text{Capacity Utilisation}
=
\frac{\text{Actual Output}}{\text{Potential Output}}
\times 100
\]
$$
### Time variance
$$
\[
\text{Time Variance}
=
\text{Actual Time}
-
\text{Planned Time}
\]
$$
***

## 59. Effectiveness Formulas

### Target achievement
$$
\[
\text{Target Achievement}
=
\frac{\text{Actual Achievement}}
{\text{Planned Achievement}}
\times 100
\]
$$
### Coverage
$$
\[
\text{Coverage}
=
\frac{\text{Beneficiaries Served}}
{\text{Eligible Beneficiaries}}
\times 100
\]
$$
### Functional asset rate
$$
\[
\text{Functional Asset Rate}
=
\frac{\text{Functional Assets}}
{\text{Total Assets Created}}
\times 100
\]
$$
### Outcome gap
$$
\[
\text{Outcome Gap}
=
\text{Target Outcome}
-
\text{Actual Outcome}
\]
$$
A formula is only as reliable as the underlying data. Auditors must validate the data before calculating indicators.

# Part XV: Final Summary Table

## 60. The 3Es in One Example

Suppose a municipality spends ₹1 crore on a drainage project.

| Question | Audit focus | Possible finding |
|---|---|---|
| Was material bought at a reasonable price? | Economy | Materials purchased 25% above comparable rates |
| Was the project completed with reasonable resources and time? | Efficiency | Project took 18 months instead of 9 months and used excess material |
| Did flooding reduce in the target area? | Effectiveness | Flooding reduced only marginally because the outfall was not completed |

### Overall conclusion

The project may have failed all three tests:

- **Not economical** because inputs were purchased at excessive cost.
- **Not efficient** because construction was delayed and resources were wasted.
- **Not effective** because the intended flood-reduction result was not achieved.

***

## 61. Conclusion

The 3Es provide a practical way to examine whether public money has produced value.

- **Economy** examines the cost and quality of inputs.
- **Efficiency** examines the relationship between resources and outputs.
- **Effectiveness** examines whether intended objectives and outcomes were achieved.

A proper 3E audit should not stop at asking whether money was spent or whether vouchers were available. It should follow the complete chain:

```text
Was the input purchased reasonably?
        ↓
Was it used productively?
        ↓
Was the intended public result achieved?
```

For local-body and DLFA work, the 3Es are especially useful in auditing:

- Public works.
- Water supply.
- Sanitation.
- Waste management.
- Revenue collection.
- Grants.
- Streetlighting.
- Municipal services.
- Panchayat development programmes.
- Asset creation and utilisation.

The strongest performance audit is evidence-based, objective, balanced and constructive. It identifies not only what went wrong, but also why it went wrong and how the local authority can improve.
