# The Birth of Local Fund Audit in India and Sikkim

Local fund audit is the examination of money managed by local authorities such as municipalities, gram panchayats, zilla panchayats and other local institutions. Its purpose is to verify whether public money was properly collected, recorded, authorised, spent and protected.

In simple terms, **local fund audit checks whether local governments have used local and government funds lawfully, economically and for the intended public purpose**.

The history of local fund audit in India is closely connected with:

- The growth of municipalities and local boards during the colonial period.
- The need to control local taxes and public funds.
- The development of Panchayati Raj institutions.
- The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments.
- The recommendations of the Finance Commissions.
- The role of the Comptroller and Auditor General of India.
- State-level Local Fund Audit Directorates.

Sikkim has its own distinctive history. Its local administration developed from the traditional village system of the Namgyal period, followed by the Sikkim Panchayat Act, 1965, the Sikkim Panchayat Act, 1993 and finally the Sikkim Local Fund Audit Act, 2012.

***

## 1. What Is a Local Fund?

Before discussing the history, it is important to understand the expression **local fund**.

A local fund generally means money that is legally controlled or administered by a local authority. It may include:

- Property tax.
- House tax.
- Water charges.
- Sanitation cess.
- Land revenue shared with the local body.
- Market fees.
- Licence fees.
- User charges.
- Government grants.
- Finance Commission grants.
- Scheme funds.
- Contributions from residents.
- Loans and deposits.
- Income from local assets.

For example, if a municipality collects property tax and uses it to maintain roads, drains and streetlights, that money forms part of the municipality’s local fund.

Similarly, if a gram panchayat receives a government grant for drinking water or rural roads, the grant becomes part of the fund administered by that local body.

### Simple explanation

A local fund is not necessarily money raised only by the local body. It may also include money received from the State or Union Government if the local authority is responsible for managing and spending it.

***

## 2. Why Did Local Fund Audit Become Necessary?

Local governments handle money close to the citizens. They construct roads, maintain sanitation, operate markets, provide water services and implement welfare programmes.

This creates several risks:

- Money may be spent without proper authority.
- Revenue may be collected but not deposited.
- Cash may be misappropriated.
- Government grants may be diverted.
- Works may be paid for without completion.
- Assets may not be recorded.
- Procurement may be irregular.
- Unspent balances may remain unexplained.
- Accounts may not be prepared on time.
- Officials may fail to maintain supporting vouchers.

Local fund audit developed to address these risks.

### Main objectives

A local fund audit generally seeks to determine whether:

1. The accounts are complete and accurate.
2. Every receipt has been properly recorded.
3. Every payment is supported by a voucher.
4. Expenditure is legally authorised.
5. Funds are used for the approved purpose.
6. Assets and stores physically exist.
7. Losses and shortages have been reported.
8. Internal controls are functioning.
9. Irregularities have been corrected.
10. Public money has been protected.

The audit is therefore not merely an exercise in checking figures. It is also a system of public accountability.

***

## 3. Early Development in British India

The modern system of local fund audit developed during the colonial period when municipalities, local boards and other local institutions began collecting taxes and administering public services.

The growth of local taxation created a need for a formal audit mechanism. Local authorities were given responsibility for funds, but the government also needed assurance that these funds were not misused.

One early example was the **Madras Local Fund Act of 1871**. Historical records describe how local funds in the Madras Presidency were constituted and administered under this law. The accounts of local fund boards were examined by government accounting authorities, including the Accountant-General for several categories of expenditure. [archive](https://archive.org/details/dli.csl.7900)

### Simple explanation

The government realised that local bodies could not be allowed to collect and spend taxes without financial checking. Therefore, audit arrangements were gradually introduced through provincial laws.

***

## 4. The Beginning of Local Audit in Bengal

The history of local audit in Bengal is particularly important for present-day West Bengal and the eastern region of India.

According to the historical account published by the Comptroller and Auditor General of India, the **Local Audit Department in Bengal was first created in 1880**. Its purpose was to audit the accounts of municipalities and other local funds.

The department was headed by an officer known as the **Examiner of Local Accounts**, who was attached to the office of the Accountant-General. Over time, the department’s responsibility expanded to include:

- Local funds.
- Municipal institutions.
- Certain non-governmental institutions.
- Some government institutions.
- Other bodies whose accounts required independent examination.

 [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/uploads/cag_pdf/thematic_history/chap_14.pdf)

### What does this mean?

In 1880, Bengal created a specialised arrangement for examining local government accounts. This was an important step because it separated local fund auditing from ordinary administrative supervision.

Instead of relying only on the local authority itself, an external officer examined the accounts.

***

## 5. Local Audit After the Reforms of 1921

The Government of India reforms of 1921 changed the constitutional and administrative relationship between the central and provincial governments.

After these reforms, the audit of local bodies became a **provincial subject**. In Bengal, the government decided that administrative control of the Local Audit Department would remain with the Auditor-General.

This arrangement helped preserve a degree of professional independence in auditing local funds. The Examiner of Local Accounts continued to audit local institutions under the broader framework of the Accountant-General’s office.

### Simple explanation

Before 1921, local audit was closely connected with the centralised colonial accounting structure. After the reforms, provinces received greater responsibility, but Bengal retained professional audit control under the Auditor-General.

***

## 6. Local Fund Audit in the Pre-Independence Period

During the pre-independence period, local fund audit was primarily concerned with:

- Checking whether expenditure was authorised.
- Verifying tax collections.
- Examining cashbooks.
- Checking vouchers.
- Detecting fraud and embezzlement.
- Verifying stores and stock.
- Reviewing outstanding dues.
- Reporting illegal payments.
- Identifying losses caused by negligence.

The system was often legalistic and transaction-based. In other words, the auditor mainly checked whether the local authority had followed the rules.

Modern concepts such as performance audit, value-for-money audit, outcome audit and risk-based audit became more prominent much later.

***

## 7. The Bihar and Odisha Local Fund Audit Act, 1925

Another important stage in the development of local fund audit was the **Bihar and Odisha Local Fund Audit Act, 1925**.

The Odisha Local Fund Audit Manual records that the Local Fund Audit Organisation originated under this Act during the pre-independence period. The organisation was attached to the then Accountant-General’s Office. [finance.odisha.gov](https://finance.odisha.gov.in/sites/default/files/2020-02/ODISHA_LOCAL_FUND_AUDIT_MANUAL_2014.pdf)

This example is useful because it shows how local fund audit was developing through provincial legislation across British India.

### Important features of the early model

The early local fund audit system generally contained the following elements:

- A statutory authority to audit.
- A designated Examiner of Local Accounts.
- A legal obligation on local bodies to submit accounts.
- Authority to demand documents.
- Authority to question responsible officials.
- Preparation of audit reports.
- Reporting of illegal payments and losses.
- Recovery mechanisms for certain losses.
- An appeal procedure.
- Government rules prescribing audit procedure.

These features later influenced local fund audit laws in several Indian States.

***

## 8. The Odisha Local Fund Audit Act, 1948

After independence, States began replacing colonial-era arrangements with their own laws.

In Odisha, the **Odisha Local Fund Audit Act, 1948** was enacted to consolidate and unify the law relating to the audit of local funds. It came into force on **1 June 1952**. [finance.odisha.gov](https://finance.odisha.gov.in/sites/default/files/2020-02/ODISHA_LOCAL_FUND_AUDIT_MANUAL_2014.pdf)

The law provided a statutory framework for:

- Declaring local authorities subject to audit.
- Appointing an Examiner of Local Accounts.
- Appointing auditors.
- Requiring local authorities to produce documents.
- Preparing audit reports.
- Reporting illegal payments and losses.
- Surcharging responsible persons.
- Recovering amounts as arrears of land revenue.
- Allowing appeals against surcharge orders.
- Making rules for audit procedure.

### Example of surcharge

Suppose an officer authorises an illegal payment of ₹50,000 without any legal authority or supporting documentation. If the payment was caused by negligence or misconduct, the competent audit authority may initiate surcharge proceedings.

If the amount is finally certified as recoverable and is not paid, the law may permit recovery as an arrear of land revenue.

### Why this is important

This illustrates that local fund audit is not limited to issuing observations. In some statutory systems, audit findings may lead to personal financial liability.

***

## 9. Growth of Local Fund Audit Organisations

After independence, several States established their own Local Fund Audit Organisations. These organisations were generally placed under the Finance Department or another government department.

Their names differed from State to State, including:

- Directorate of Local Fund Audit.
- Examiner of Local Accounts.
- Local Fund Audit Department.
- Local Bodies Audit Directorate.
- Panchayat Audit Organisation.
- Municipal Audit Department.

Their functions were broadly similar:

- Audit local authority accounts.
- Examine receipts and expenditure.
- Verify cash and stores.
- Report losses and irregularities.
- Conduct special audits.
- Monitor correction of objections.
- Support financial discipline.
- Prepare consolidated audit reports.

### Example: Odisha

The Odisha Local Fund Audit Manual describes a historical structure in which separate audit organisations initially examined different categories of local institutions. These organisations were later brought under unified administrative control.

The audit structure included district audit offices, audit superintendents and auditors. The organisation’s jurisdiction extended to panchayats, municipalities, development authorities, universities, educational institutions and other bodies. [finance.odisha.gov](https://finance.odisha.gov.in/sites/default/files/2020-02/ODISHA_LOCAL_FUND_AUDIT_MANUAL_2014.pdf)

### Simple explanation

In many States, local fund audit evolved from separate departmental arrangements into a unified Finance Department-controlled organisation.

***

## 10. Constitutional Recognition of Local Government

A major change occurred through the **73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendment Acts, 1992**.

The amendments received Presidential assent on **20 April 1993**. They inserted:

- **Part IX** of the Constitution for Panchayats.
- **Part IX-A** for Municipalities.

Part IX contains Articles 243 to 243-O, while Part IX-A contains Articles 243-P to 243-ZG. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/uploads/cag_pdf/thematic_history/chap_14.pdf)

The 73rd Amendment dealt with rural local government, while the 74th Amendment dealt with urban local government.

### The 29 subjects of Panchayats

The Eleventh Schedule contains **29 subjects** that may be assigned to Panchayats. These include areas such as:

- Agriculture.
- Land improvement.
- Minor irrigation.
- Rural housing.
- Drinking water.
- Roads.
- Rural electrification.
- Poverty alleviation.
- Education.
- Health and sanitation.
- Women and child development.
- Public distribution system.
- Maintenance of community assets.

### The 18 subjects of Municipalities

The Twelfth Schedule contains **18 functions** for municipalities, including:

- Urban planning.
- Regulation of land use.
- Roads and bridges.
- Water supply.
- Public health.
- Sanitation.
- Solid waste management.
- Fire services.
- Urban forestry.
- Slum improvement.
- Urban poverty alleviation.
- Public amenities.
- Registration of births and deaths.

### Why did this affect audit?

When local bodies receive more functions and funds, the need for audit becomes greater.

The constitutional amendments created stronger local institutions, but they also created a larger accountability challenge:

> Who will audit the money received and spent by Panchayats and Municipalities?

***

## 11. Articles 243J and 243Z

The Constitution does not itself prescribe one uniform audit organisation for all local bodies.

Instead:

- **Article 243J** allows the State Legislature to make provisions for maintaining Panchayat accounts and auditing those accounts.
- **Article 243Z** provides a similar framework for municipalities.

This means that State laws determine the detailed audit arrangements.

### Simple explanation

The Constitution recognises the need for local-body accounting and audit, but each State generally creates the operational system through its own legislation.

That is why local fund audit laws differ between States.

***

## 12. The Finance Commission and Local Body Audit

Finance Commissions played a major role in strengthening local fund audit.

The **Eleventh Finance Commission** expressed concern about weaknesses in local-body accounting and audit. It recommended stronger arrangements, including:

- A greater role for the CAG in supervising local-body accounts and audit.
- Technical guidance and supervision over Directors of Local Fund Audit.
- Standard formats for local-body budgets and accounts.
- Better computerisation of accounting data.
- Improved audit standards.
- Legislative examination of local-body audit reports.
- Better follow-up of audit findings.

The CAG’s historical account records that the Eleventh Finance Commission recommended that local fund audit agencies should work under the technical guidance and supervision of the CAG. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/uploads/cag_pdf/thematic_history/chap_14.pdf)

### Why was this necessary?

Many local bodies suffered from:

- Shortage of trained accounting staff.
- Weak record keeping.
- Outdated accounting formats.
- Poor maintenance of registers.
- Lack of reliable financial data.
- Weak follow-up of audit objections.
- Inadequate response to audit reports.

### Simple explanation

Finance Commissions understood that giving money to local bodies was not enough. There also had to be a reliable system to record, verify and report how that money was used.

***

## 13. The Role of the CAG

The Comptroller and Auditor General of India has several possible roles in local-body audit, depending on the legal framework of the State.

The CAG may:

- Conduct audit under the CAG’s Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service Act, 1971.
- Conduct audit of bodies substantially financed by government revenues.
- Receive entrustment under Section 20(1) of the CAG’s Act.
- Provide technical guidance and supervision to the State Local Fund Audit Directorate.
- Conduct supplementary or test audit.
- Prescribe accounting and budget formats.
- Develop audit standards and manuals.
- Provide training to local-body auditors.
- Report important findings to the State Legislature.

### Section 20(1) arrangement

Under Section 20(1) of the CAG’s Act, the President, Governor or Administrator may request the CAG to audit the accounts of a body or authority on agreed terms and conditions.

In Sikkim, the State Government entrusted the audit of Panchayati Raj Institutions to the CAG in **June 2011** under Section 20(1), through a Technical Guidance and Supervision arrangement. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/ag/sikkim/en/page-ag-sikkim-local-bodies-section-and-function-of-auditee-units)

### Important distinction

The CAG’s technical guidance does not always mean that the CAG becomes the primary auditor.

In many States:

- The State Local Fund Audit Directorate remains the primary auditor.
- The CAG provides technical guidance and supervision.
- The Accountant-General may conduct supplementary checks.
- Audit findings may be reported through different statutory channels.

***

## 14. The West Bengal Position

West Bengal has a particularly old local audit tradition.

The CAG records that:

- The Local Audit Department of Bengal was created in **1880**.
- It initially audited municipalities and other local funds.
- The Examiner of Local Accounts was attached to the Accountant-General.
- The arrangement continued after the reforms of 1921.
- The Examiner of Local Accounts in West Bengal audits Panchayati Raj Institutions and Urban Local Bodies.
- The Accountant-General conducted **100 per cent audit of Gram Panchayat accounts from 2002–03 onwards** under the West Bengal Panchayat Act, 1973. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/uploads/cag_pdf/thematic_history/chap_14.pdf)

### Why West Bengal is significant

West Bengal is one of the States where the CAG institution has historically played a direct role in local-body audit.

This differs from the model followed in many other States, where a separate Director of Local Fund Audit operates under the State Government.

# Part II: The Birth and Development of Local Fund Audit in Sikkim

## 15. Traditional Local Administration in Sikkim

The roots of local administration in Sikkim are older than the modern Panchayati Raj system.

According to the CAG’s account of local bodies in Sikkim, the State’s traditional administration developed during the period of the Namgyal dynasty.

During that period:

- Landlords were known as **Kazis**.
- Mandals and Karbaris worked under the Kazis.
- They helped manage agricultural activities.
- They collected taxes known as **Dhuri Khazana**.

This system was not modern local self-government, but it created an early form of local administration and revenue collection. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/ag/sikkim/en/page-ag-sikkim-local-bodies-section-and-function-of-auditee-units)

### Simple explanation

Before elected panchayats existed, village-level administration in Sikkim was carried out through traditional officials and local intermediaries.

They helped manage land, agriculture and revenue collection.

***

## 16. Panchayats After the Abolition of Zamindari

After the abolition of the zamindari system in **1948**, Panchayats were created in Sikkim.

The early Panchayats consisted of:

- The former landlord or his representative.
- Four other members from the block.
- Members selected by people in a meeting convened for that purpose.

These Panchayats helped fill the administrative gap created by the abolition of zamindari.

However, they were initially more like **quasi-judicial or Nyaya Panchayats** than fully developed democratic local governments. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/ag/sikkim/en/page-ag-sikkim-local-bodies-section-and-function-of-auditee-units)

### What does “quasi-judicial” mean?

A quasi-judicial body performs some functions similar to a court. It may help resolve local disputes or decide certain matters, even though it is not a regular court.

***

## 17. The Sikkim Panchayat Act, 1965

The **Sikkim Panchayat Act, 1965** was enacted to consolidate and amend the laws relating to Panchayats in Sikkim.

It became effective in **December 1965**. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/ag/sikkim/en/page-ag-sikkim-local-bodies-section-and-function-of-auditee-units)

The purpose of the Panchayat system was to:

- Promote rural development.
- Encourage participation by villagers.
- Provide a local administrative structure.
- Give communities a role in village-level decision-making.
- Mobilise local resources.

### Term and functions

Under the 1965 arrangement:

- The term of a Panchayat was **three years**.
- Each Panchayat was assigned **16 duties and functions**.

The functions required financial resources. The Panchayats received money from several sources.

### Sources of Panchayat revenue

The resources included:

- House tax.
- A proportion of land revenue.
- **10 per cent of the land revenue of the block**.
- Matching grants from the Darbar for original works.
- Public contributions.
- Sanitation cess.
- Water cess.

 [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/ag/sikkim/en/page-ag-sikkim-local-bodies-section-and-function-of-auditee-units)

### Why is this relevant to audit?

Once a Panchayat receives taxes, grants and cesses, it must maintain accounts. Once it maintains accounts, an audit mechanism becomes necessary.

The 1965 Act therefore represents an important stage in the development of local financial administration in Sikkim, even though the specific modern Local Fund Audit Act came later.

***

## 18. The Sikkim Panchayat Act, 1993

The **Sikkim Panchayat Act, 1993** was enacted in the context of the 73rd Constitutional Amendment.

It provided a modern legal framework for Panchayati Raj Institutions in Sikkim.

The 1993 law aligned Sikkim’s Panchayat system with the constitutional framework for:

- Gram Panchayats.
- Zilla Panchayats.
- Village-level democratic participation.
- Local development.
- Decentralisation.
- Maintenance and audit of accounts.

The CAG records that Section 48 of the Sikkim Panchayat Act, 1993 dealt with the audit of Gram Panchayat accounts. Section 48(2) required the State Government to appoint an auditor, while Section 48(3) provided for audit by the CAG. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/ag/sikkim/en/page-ag-sikkim-local-bodies-section-and-function-of-auditee-units)

### Significance

The 1993 Act made audit an explicit part of the Panchayat governance framework.

This was important because Panchayats were no longer merely local administrative units. They were constitutional institutions of self-government.

***

## 19. Sikkim Municipal Legislation

The 74th Constitutional Amendment required an equivalent framework for urban local bodies.

Sikkim enacted the **Sikkim Municipalities Act, 2007**, which created the legal basis for urban local-government institutions such as:

- Municipal Corporations.
- Municipal Councils.
- Nagar Panchayats.

The Sikkim Local Fund Audit Act, 2012 later included municipal corporations, municipalities and other specified urban bodies within the audit framework.

### Simple explanation

The Panchayat system covered rural areas, while municipal legislation covered urban areas. The Local Fund Audit Act was later designed to cover both categories.

***

## 20. Sikkim and the CAG’s Technical Guidance

In accordance with the recommendations of the Eleventh Finance Commission and guidance issued by the Ministry of Finance, the Government of Sikkim entrusted the audit of Panchayati Raj Institutions to the CAG in **June 2011**.

The entrustment was made under **Section 20(1) of the CAG’s Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service Act, 1971**.

The arrangement was based on **Technical Guidance and Supervision**, commonly called TGS. [cag.gov](https://cag.gov.in/ag/sikkim/en/page-ag-sikkim-local-bodies-section-and-function-of-auditee-units)

### What does TGS mean?

Technical Guidance and Supervision means that:

- The State’s own audit authority may continue to conduct the primary audit.
- The CAG provides professional guidance.
- The CAG may review audit quality.
- Audit methodology and standards may be improved.
- Training and documentation may be strengthened.
- Supplementary audit may be conducted where legally permitted.

This arrangement aims to combine State-level administrative responsibility with professional audit oversight.

***

## 21. The Sikkim Local Fund Audit Act, 2012

The modern statutory birth of local fund audit in Sikkim occurred with the **Sikkim Local Fund Audit Act, 2012**.

Important dates are:

- The Act was passed as **Act No. 22 of 2012**.
- The Governor gave assent on **3 May 2012**.
- The Act was dated **21 June 2012**.
- It was published in the Sikkim Gazette on **30 June 2012**.
- The Directorate of Local Fund Audit was established in **June 2012**. [prsindia](https://prsindia.org/files/bills_acts/acts_states/sikkim/2012/2012Sikkim22.pdf)

The Act’s long title states that it was enacted:

> To provide for and regulate the audit of local funds under the management or control of certain local authorities in the State of Sikkim.

### Simple explanation

The 2012 Act created a clear legal system for deciding:

- Who will audit local funds.
- Which institutions must be audited.
- What records auditors can demand.
- How often audits should be conducted.
- What an audit report must contain.
- How audit objections should be answered.
- How reports should reach the Government and Legislature.

***

***

## 23. Section 1: Short Title, Extent and Commencement

Section 1 provides that the law is called the **Sikkim Local Fund Audit Act, 2012**.

It extends to the whole of Sikkim.

The Act comes into force from the date notified by the State Government in the Official Gazette.

### Simple meaning

The Act applies across the State, but the Government must formally notify the date on which it becomes operational.

***

## 24. Section 2: Important Definitions

Section 2 defines important expressions used throughout the Act.

### Auditor

The term includes:

- The Director of Local Fund Audit.
- Officers of the Local Fund Audit Directorate authorised by the Director.

### Concurrent audit

Concurrent audit means continuous or ongoing audit of accounts rather than an audit conducted only after the financial year ends.

### Detailed audit

Detailed audit means examination of all transactions for the entire year or for a specified period.

### Special audit

Special audit means a focused audit of:

- A particular item.
- A group of transactions.
- A suspected irregularity.
- A period already audited.
- A matter requiring thorough examination.

### Local authority

The definition includes:

- Municipal Corporations.
- Municipal Councils.
- Nagar Panchayats.
- Gram Panchayats.
- Zilla Panchayats.
- District Planning Committees.
- Other authorities, bodies or institutions administering a local fund.

### Local fund

A local fund means:

- A fund administered by a local authority.
- A fund established under law or government order.
- Any other fund declared by Government to be a local fund.

### Simple meaning

The definitions are important because they establish the scope of the Act. They clarify who may be audited and what money is treated as a local fund.

***

## 25. Section 3: Appointment of the Director

Section 3 provides for appointment of the **Director of Local Fund Audit**.

The State Government appoints a qualified person as Director.

The Director is responsible for the audit of the local authorities and local funds included in the Schedule.

The Government may also appoint other officers and staff to assist the Director.

All officers of the Directorate work under the general supervision and control of the Director.

### Simple meaning

The Director is the head of the Sikkim Local Fund Audit system.

The Director is not merely an administrative officer. The office has statutory responsibility for conducting and supervising local-body audits.

***

## 26. Section 4: Audit of Accounts

Section 4 makes the Director responsible for auditing the accounts of local authorities and local funds included in the Schedule.

The audit must be conducted according to the procedure provided by the Act and rules.

The Act also recognises the relationship with the CAG by making the Director’s powers subject to the CAG’s governing law.

### Simple meaning

The Director is the primary statutory auditor for the institutions listed in the Act.

However, the State audit system must operate consistently with the legal powers and responsibilities of the CAG.

***

## 27. Section 5: Audit of Other Institutions

The Director may audit an authority, body, institution or fund not included in the Schedule if:

- The State Government gives prior sanction.
- The institution agrees to the terms and conditions.
- The audit is undertaken according to the approved arrangement.

### Example

Suppose a government-created development authority is not initially included in the Schedule. The State Government may authorise the Director to audit it.

### Simple meaning

The audit system can be expanded to cover additional institutions when required.

***

## 28. Section 6: Stock, Stores and Cash Verification

Section 6 gives auditors authority to examine:

- Stock.
- Stores.
- Materials.
- Equipment.
- Other assets held by the local authority.

The Director may seek the help of a technical expert where necessary.

An officer not below the rank of Deputy Director may conduct surprise inspections of stock and stores.

The Director or auditor must verify the cash balance during audit and inspection.

### Why this matters

Financial audit is not limited to books. A local authority may show ten computers in its asset register, but only eight may physically exist.

Similarly, a stock register may show 500 bags of cement, while the actual stock may be much lower.

Physical verification helps detect:

- Shortage.
- Misappropriation.
- Obsolete stock.
- Unrecorded assets.
- False purchases.
- Poor custody arrangements.

***

## 29. Section 7: Frequency and Manner of Audit

The Act provides that the auditor shall conduct audit annually.

The Director may also arrange:

- Concurrent audit.
- Special audit.
- Audit at intervals considered necessary.
- Audit required under another governing law.

### Simple meaning

The normal cycle is annual audit, but higher-risk institutions may require continuous or special audit.

For example:

- A local body handling large construction grants may require more frequent review.
- A suspected cash shortage may justify a special audit.
- A body with weak accounting systems may need concurrent audit.

***

## 30. Section 8: Auditor as a Public Servant

An auditor acting under the Act is treated as a public servant for the purposes of the powers and duties conferred by the Act.

### Why this matters

This provision gives legal recognition to the auditor’s official role.

It also reinforces that:

- The auditor is performing a public function.
- Interference with the audit process is serious.
- The auditor must act honestly and within the law.
- The auditor’s work relates to protection of public money.

***

## 31. Section 9: Responsibility to Prepare Accounts

Section 9 places responsibility on the executive authority of the local body to prepare accounts.

Accounts relating to a financial year must be:

- Prepared in the prescribed form.
- Prepared in the prescribed manner.
- Presented for audit within **three months after the close of the financial year**.

### Example

If the financial year ends on 31 March 2026, the accounts should generally be presented for audit within three months, subject to the prescribed rules and applicable arrangements.

### Simple meaning

The local body cannot wait indefinitely before sending its accounts for audit.

Timely preparation is necessary because delayed accounts reduce the usefulness of audit and make follow-up difficult.

***

## 32. Section 10: Completion of Audit

The auditor must complete the audit within **six months from the date on which the accounts are presented for audit**.

### Why this is important

This creates a time discipline for the audit department.

However, timely completion also depends on the local body:

- Producing complete records.
- Providing vouchers.
- Replying to audit requisitions.
- Making responsible officers available.
- Preparing reconciliations.
- Maintaining registers correctly.

### Practical example

If accounts are presented on 1 July, the audit should ordinarily be completed within six months from that date, subject to the Act, rules and practical circumstances.

***

## 33. Section 11: Power to Require Documents and Attendance

Section 11 provides important audit powers.

The auditor may require production of:

- Vouchers.
- Statements.
- Returns.
- Correspondence.
- Notes.
- Registers.
- Supporting documents.
- Other records necessary for audit.

The auditor may also require:

- Employees responsible for records to appear.
- Persons connected with contracts to answer questions.
- Officials to prepare statements.
- Officers or members to provide written explanations.

A reasonable time of not less than **three days** may be allowed for compliance with a requisition.

The auditor should normally provide at least **two weeks’ written notice** before starting an audit.

However, shorter notice or an audit without notice may be allowed for special reasons, special audit or detailed audit under the Director’s order.

### Simple meaning

The auditor has the right to obtain the information needed to verify the accounts.

The local body must cooperate with the audit.

***

## 34. Section 12: Sending the Audit Report

After completing the audit, the auditor must send the report to the local authority.

The report should be sent as soon as practicable and generally not later than **three months after completion of the audit**.

Copies may also be sent to:

- Government officers.
- Controlling authorities.
- Statutory bodies.
- Other bodies specified by Government.
- Authorities mentioned in the law governing the local body.

### Simple meaning

The audit report is not meant to remain only in the auditor’s office. It must reach the local authority and the relevant supervisory authorities.

***

## 35. Section 13: Contents of the Audit Report

The audit report must include significant irregularities.

These include:

### Illegal payments

Payments that appear to be contrary to law.

**Example:** Payment made without sanction from the competent authority.

### Loss caused by negligence or misconduct

The amount of deficiency or loss caused by an official or other responsible person.

**Example:** A cashier fails to deposit collections and the money is lost.

### Misappropriation or misuse

Cases where local funds have been diverted, misused or misappropriated.

**Example:** A grant sanctioned for drinking water is used for an unrelated expenditure.

### Receipts not accounted for

Money received but not entered in the accounts.

**Example:** Market fees collected from vendors but not deposited in the local fund.

### Material irregularity

Any other serious accounting, financial or administrative irregularity.

**Example:** A large work is split into several smaller purchases to avoid tender requirements.

### Simple explanation

The audit report must focus on matters that can affect public money, legality, accountability and financial control.

***

## 36. Section 14: Action After the Audit Report

After receiving the report, the executive authority must either:

- Correct the defects and irregularities; or
- Submit an explanation to the Director.

This must normally be done within **three months** of receiving the report, or within the period allowed under the applicable law.

The auditor may then:

- Accept the explanation and drop the objection.
- Reject the explanation.
- Hold that the irregularity has not been corrected.
- Continue the objection for further action.

The final position must be communicated to the local authority.

The local authority must publish specified portions of unresolved audit findings in its next administration report.

The relevant reports and explanations must also be open for public inspection for **one month**.

### Why this is important

This section creates an audit follow-up system.

An audit report is useful only when the local body:

1. Accepts the observation.
2. Corrects the mistake.
3. Recovers the amount, where necessary.
4. Strengthens the system.
5. Provides evidence of compliance.

***

## 37. Section 15: Powers and Duties of the Director

The Director has general supervision and control over auditors working under the Act.

The Director may condone a small payment contrary to law if:

- There was no negligence or misconduct.
- The amount does not exceed ₹100 in each case.

### Simple meaning

The provision allows the Director to avoid excessive formal action for very small technical payments where there is no misconduct.

However, the power does not excuse fraud, deliberate violation or negligence.

***

## 38. Section 16: Delegation of Powers

The Director may delegate powers and functions to officers of the Local Fund Audit Directorate.

The Director may also withdraw the delegated powers.

### Simple meaning

The Director does not personally have to perform every audit function. Specific responsibilities can be assigned to authorised officers.

This supports:

- District-level audit.
- Special audits.
- Inspection.
- Document verification.
- Report preparation.
- Follow-up of objections.

***

## 39. Section 17: Dispensing with Detailed Audit

The Director may dispense with a detailed audit of:

- Particular accounts.
- A class of transactions.
- Certain low-risk areas.

A limited check may be applied instead.

### Important caution

This does not mean that the transaction is ignored entirely. It means that the extent of checking may be reduced based on circumstances.

A limited check may be appropriate where:

- The transaction is low-risk.
- Internal controls are strong.
- The same area has been recently audited.
- Resources are limited.
- A risk-based audit plan is being followed.

High-risk transactions should normally receive detailed examination.

***

## 40. Section 18: Reporting of Defalcation or Loss

When a local authority discovers a loss or defalcation involving money or stores, the executive authority must promptly report it to:

- The Director.
- The controlling authority.
- The concerned departmental head.

The report must explain the circumstances that caused the loss.

After receiving the report, the Director must conduct or arrange a special audit depending on the seriousness of the loss.

The local authority may also initiate criminal proceedings where appropriate.

### Simple explanation

The local body must not hide a loss until the next annual audit.

Losses must be reported immediately so that:

- Evidence can be protected.
- Responsibility can be fixed.
- Recovery can be attempted.
- Criminal action can be considered.
- Similar weaknesses can be corrected.

***

## 41. Section 19: Laying of the Audit Report Before the Legislature

The Director must send an annual consolidated report to the Government.

The Government must cause the report to be laid before the Sikkim Legislative Assembly within **three months of receiving it**.

### Why this matters

Laying the report before the Legislature gives audit findings a constitutional and democratic accountability channel.

It allows elected representatives to examine:

- Financial irregularities.
- Delays in accounts.
- Misuse of grants.
- Weak internal controls.
- Unresolved audit objections.
- Repeated failures by local authorities.

### Simple explanation

The final responsibility for public accountability does not stop at the local authority. Serious audit findings can reach the State Legislature.

***

## 42. Section 20: Protection for Good-Faith Action

The Act protects the Director and authorised officers from legal proceedings for actions taken in good faith under the Act or rules.

This protection is not a licence for arbitrary behaviour.

It applies to lawful actions performed honestly within the scope of official duties.

***

## 43. Section 21: Pending Audits

Audits pending when the Act commenced may continue and be completed under the applicable transitional arrangements.

### Simple meaning

The commencement of a new law does not automatically cancel old pending audits.

This prevents confusion and ensures continuity.

***

## 44. Section 22: Rule-Making Power

The State Government may make rules for implementing the Act.

Rules may prescribe:

- The form of accounts.
- The manner of presenting accounts.
- Audit procedure.
- The duties of auditors.
- The frequency of audit.
- Publication requirements.
- Other matters needed to operate the Act.

### Why rules are necessary

An Act usually establishes the legal framework. Detailed rules explain how the framework operates in practice.

For example, rules may prescribe:

- Registers to be maintained.
- Formats for cashbooks.
- Forms for audit reports.
- Time limits for replies.
- Procedures for physical verification.
- Methods of recording audit objections.

***

## 45. Section 23: Removal of Difficulties

If difficulties arise in implementing the Act, the Government may issue orders to remove those difficulties, provided the orders are consistent with the Act.

### Simple meaning

This is a transitional and administrative provision that helps the Government deal with practical problems during implementation.

***

## 46. Section 24: Savings

The Act preserves pending cases, appeals, suits and proceedings that began before the Act commenced.

### Simple meaning

Existing legal proceedings continue under the law applicable when they began.

## 49. Relationship Between DLFA and CAG in Sikkim

Sikkim follows a model involving both the State Local Fund Audit Directorate and the CAG.

### Director of Local Fund Audit

The DLFA is the primary State-level auditor responsible for conducting local-body audits under the Sikkim Local Fund Audit Act, 2012.

### Comptroller and Auditor General

The CAG may:

- Provide technical guidance and supervision.
- Review the quality of local-body audit.
- Conduct supplementary audit where authorised.
- Support audit standards and methodology.
- Examine local-body accounts under relevant constitutional and statutory provisions.

### The practical distinction

The DLFA performs the principal statutory audit function under State law.

The CAG provides a higher-level professional and constitutional accountability framework.

This arrangement can be compared to a system in which:

- The State audit authority performs the regular examination.
- The CAG provides technical standards and oversight.
- The Legislature receives consolidated information for accountability.

## 52. Why Local Fund Audit Is Important for Public Administration

Local fund audit performs several important functions.

### Legal accountability

It checks whether expenditure was authorised by law, rules, budgets and competent authorities.

### Financial accountability

It verifies whether public funds were received, recorded and spent correctly.

### Administrative accountability

It examines whether officers maintained registers, vouchers, cashbooks and supporting records.

### Asset accountability

It checks whether assets purchased from public money exist and are properly recorded.

### Scheme accountability

It determines whether grants were used for the purpose for which they were released.

### Democratic accountability

It provides information to elected representatives, the Government and the public.

### Corrective accountability

It helps local authorities correct weaknesses and improve internal control.

***

## 53. Local Fund Audit Is More Than Fault-Finding

A good local fund audit should not be treated merely as a fault-finding exercise.

It should help local bodies:

- Improve accounting systems.
- Prevent recurring mistakes.
- Strengthen cash controls.
- Improve procurement.
- Maintain updated registers.
- Protect assets.
- Monitor grants.
- Ensure timely bank reconciliation.
- Improve budget control.
- Fix responsibility for losses.
- Increase public confidence.

The Odisha Local Fund Audit Manual explains that audit should disclose fraud, misappropriation, defalcation and misuse of public funds, but it should also guide local authorities towards prudent financial administration. [finance.odisha.gov](https://finance.odisha.gov.in/sites/default/files/2020-02/ODISHA_LOCAL_FUND_AUDIT_MANUAL_2014.pdf)

### Simple illustration

If an audit finds that a Panchayat repeatedly fails to reconcile its bank account, the best audit response is not only to report the defect.

It should also recommend:

- Monthly bank reconciliation.
- Certification by the Secretary.
- Review by the elected head.
- Separate recording of uncleared cheques.
- Monitoring of old outstanding items.
- Follow-up in the next audit.

## 55. Difference Between Local Fund Audit and CAG Audit

The two systems may overlap, but they are not always identical.

| Point | Local Fund Audit | CAG Audit |
|---|---|---|
| Legal basis | State local fund audit law | Constitution, CAG’s Act and relevant State laws |
| Main focus | Regular statutory audit of local accounts | Public-sector accountability, supplementary audit, compliance, performance and financial audit |
| Primary authority | State Local Fund Audit Directorate or equivalent | Comptroller and Auditor General |
| Administrative control | Usually State Government | CAG institution |
| Technical standards | State rules and manuals, often influenced by CAG | CAG auditing standards and manuals |
| Coverage | Institutions specified by State law or notification | Coverage depends on constitutional and statutory authority |
| Reporting | Local authority, Government and Legislature as prescribed | Governor, Legislature and other authorities as applicable |

### Important point

The exact relationship depends on the law of the particular State. Therefore, audit officers should always consult:

- The State Local Fund Audit Act.
- Panchayat legislation.
- Municipal legislation.
- CAG entrustment orders.
- Finance Department notifications.
- Audit rules and manuals.

## 58. Key Lessons From the History

The history of local fund audit in India and Sikkim gives several important lessons.

### Audit follows devolution

When responsibility and money move from higher levels of government to local bodies, audit arrangements must also be strengthened.

### Law is necessary but not sufficient

A Local Fund Audit Act creates powers and procedures, but effective audit also requires:

- Trained personnel.
- Updated accounting systems.
- Proper records.
- Adequate staffing.
- Timely submission of accounts.
- Strong follow-up.

### Local audit has deep historical roots

Local fund audit did not begin after the 73rd and 74th Amendments. It existed during the colonial period, including in Bengal from 1880.

The constitutional amendments expanded and democratised local government, making audit more important.

### Independence and technical quality both matter

A State Local Fund Audit Directorate needs administrative authority and sufficient independence. At the same time, technical guidance from the CAG can help improve standards and audit quality.

### Audit findings must lead to action

An unresolved audit objection becomes meaningful only when it results in:

- Correction.
- Recovery.
- Disciplinary action.
- Criminal investigation.
- System improvement.
- Legislative review.

***

## 59. Conclusion

The birth of local fund audit in India was a gradual process. It began with the growth of municipalities, local boards and tax-funded institutions during the colonial period. Bengal created its Local Audit Department in **1880**, while other provinces enacted their own local fund audit laws, such as the Bihar and Odisha legislation of **1925**.

After independence, States developed separate statutory Local Fund Audit Organisations. The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments of **1992–1993** gave Panchayats and Municipalities a stronger constitutional role. The Finance Commissions and the CAG then helped develop standard accounting, audit and technical supervision arrangements.

In Sikkim, local administration has older roots in the Namgyal-era system of Kazis, Mandals and Karbaris. Modern Panchayati Raj developed through the Sikkim Panchayat Act, 1965 and the Sikkim Panchayat Act, 1993. The decisive statutory milestone was the **Sikkim Local Fund Audit Act, 2012**, which created a comprehensive framework for auditing municipal and Panchayati Raj institutions.

The central principle is simple:

> Local self-government requires local financial accountability.

Whenever a local authority receives public money, there must be reliable accounts, independent audit, corrective action and legislative oversight.
